# Compare the efficacy and adverse event profiles of SGLT2 inhibitors versus GLP-1 receptor agonists in patients with Type 2 diabetes who also have concurrent chronic kidney disease. Extract the sample sizes and p-values from the three most cited randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on this topic.

## Short answer

No head-to-head RCTs comparing SGLT2 inhibitors with GLP-1 receptor agonists in type 2 diabetes with CKD exist, so the three most cited kidney-outcome trials are placebo-controlled: CREDENCE (n=4,401; primary composite HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.59–0.82, p=0.00001) [14][28], DAPA-CKD (n=4,304; primary composite HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.51–0.72, p<0.001) [14][27], and EMPA-KIDNEY (n=6,609; primary composite HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.64–0.82, p<0.001) [14][26]. Indirect network meta-analyses show SGLT2 inhibitors are superior to GLP-1 receptor agonists for composite renal outcomes (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.66–0.87) and hospitalization for heart failure (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.66–0.85), with no significant difference in MACE (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.94–1.13) [4]. Adverse-event profiles differ: SGLT2 inhibitors carry higher risks of genital mycotic infections (RR 3.49, 95% CI 2.63–4.55 vs GLP-1RA) and diabetic ketoacidosis (RR 2.36, 95% CI 1.33–4.17), whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists cause more gastrointestinal adverse events (12% vs 0% in one small head-to-head study) [4][5]; per-trial p-values for these safety outcomes were not reported in the available evidence.

## The Absence of Head-to-Head Evidence: Why the Premise Fails

The premise that a direct comparison of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in type 2 diabetes with chronic kidney disease can be drawn from randomized evidence fails at the first step: no such trial exists. A systematic review published in 2021 stated plainly that "no study to date has compared the effect of SGLT-2 inhibitors with that of GLP-1 RAs in type 2 DM patients with CKD" [3]. A later review of the comparative effectiveness literature reached the same conclusion, noting that "no head-to-head RCTs have directly compared SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists" [1]. And a 2025 network meta-analysis of 14 large-scale trials confirmed that "no head-to-head trials comparing SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists were included in the primary analysis" [4]. The absence is not a gap in the literature that might be filled by a single overlooked trial; it is a structural feature of the entire evidence base.

## The landmark kidney-outcome trials are all placebo-controlled

The four trials most frequently cited as the foundation of kidney-outcome evidence in this population — CREDENCE (2019), DAPA-CKD (2020), EMPA-KIDNEY (2022), and FLOW (2024) — are each placebo-controlled [14]. CREDENCE randomized patients with type 2 diabetes and diabetic nephropathy to canagliflozin or placebo; DAPA-CKD randomized patients with chronic kidney disease, with or without diabetes, to dapagliflozin or placebo; EMPA-KIDNEY randomized patients with CKD to empagliflozin or placebo; and FLOW randomized patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD to semaglutide or placebo [14]. Three of the four test SGLT2 inhibitors; only FLOW tests a GLP-1 receptor agonist. None pits one class against the other.

This design choice has a direct consequence for the network of evidence. In network meta-analyses, the comparison between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists is made indirectly, through the shared placebo arm. The network geometry is "star-shaped, with placebo serving as the common comparator node for all active treatments" [4]. In the largest such analysis, 14 trials involving 117,633 participants formed this structure: six SGLT2 inhibitor trials (EMPA-REG OUTCOME, CANVAS Program, DECLARE-TIMI 58, VERTIS CV, CREDENCE, and SCORED) and eight GLP-1 receptor agonist trials (ELIXA, LEADER, SUSTAIN-6, EXSCEL, Harmony Outcomes, REWIND, PIONEER 6, and AMPLITUDE-O), all compared against placebo [4].

## Why the placebo-controlled design shapes the evidence base

The reliance on placebo comparators has three consequences that ripple through every downstream comparison.

First, it forces the comparison to be indirect, and indirect comparisons rest on the transitivity assumption: that the placebo arms of the SGLT2 inhibitor trials and the GLP-1 receptor agonist trials are exchangeable — that is, that the populations, protocols, and outcome definitions are sufficiently similar that the relative effect of each drug against placebo can be validly compared. That assumption is strained by the actual trial populations. In the 14-trial network, the SGLT2 inhibitor node had a lower weighted mean eGFR (65.2 mL/min/1.73 m²) than the GLP-1 receptor agonist node (76.8 mL/min/1.73 m²), because the SGLT2 inhibitor node included dedicated renal outcome trials (CREDENCE, DAPA-CKD, EMPA-KIDNEY) and heart failure trials (DAPA-HF, EMPEROR-Reduced) [4]. The prevalence of established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease was higher in the GLP-1 receptor agonist trials (76.5% vs 68.3%), while a history of heart failure was more common in the SGLT2 inhibitor trials (22.4% vs 14.5%) [4]. These imbalances were addressed through prespecified subgroup analyses and meta-regression, but the authors acknowledged that "residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out" [4].

Second, the placebo-controlled design means that the comparison between classes is always a comparison of two effect estimates against a common reference, never a direct estimate. The GRADE certainty of evidence for the indirect comparison of MACE was rated Moderate, "downgraded due to indirectness, as there were no head-to-head trials included in the primary network" [4]. Only the evidence for SGLT2 inhibitors reducing hospitalization for heart failure was rated High, and that was driven by the large magnitude of effect and consistency across sensitivity analyses rather than by any direct comparison [4].

Third, the absence of a direct comparison leaves the network vulnerable to inconsistency that cannot be resolved. In the 14-trial network, the global test for inconsistency in the primary outcome of MACE was not significant (Q = 3.54, df = 2, p = 0.17), but for the secondary outcome of hospitalization for heart failure, the global test indicated potential inconsistency (Q = 6.82, df = 2, p = 0.03), driven by the varying magnitudes of benefit observed in dedicated heart failure trials compared to general diabetes trials [4]. Because no direct evidence exists for the SGLT2 inhibitor versus GLP-1 receptor agonist comparison, the consistency check "relied on the agreement between indirect estimates derived from placebo-controlled trials" — a check that cannot detect a systematic bias shared by both indirect routes [4].

## What the indirect evidence shows — and where it disagrees

The indirect comparisons do not all point in the same direction. A comprehensive network meta-analysis of 31 trials involving 98,234 participants with type 2 diabetes found that both drug classes significantly reduced the risk of kidney failure (eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m² or renal replacement therapy) compared to placebo, but found no significant differences between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in absolute risk reductions for kidney failure [14]. In contrast, a separate analysis restricted to placebo-controlled trials of either class in 32,949 individuals with type 2 diabetes and CKD reported a 21% greater reduction in a composite kidney outcome (end-stage kidney disease, decline in kidney function, albuminuria, and renal or cardiovascular death) with SGLT2 inhibitors compared to GLP-1 receptor agonists [14]. A third analysis, focused on the broader population, found that SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with greater preservation of eGFR and a lower risk of CKD progression and doubling of serum creatinine, but no significant difference in albuminuria [14].

The disagreement between these analyses is itself a product of the indirect design. Different networks include different trials, define composite outcomes differently, and apply different statistical models. The Palmer BMJ network meta-analysis and the Yamada analysis reached different conclusions about kidney failure risk reductions in part because they drew on different trial sets and different outcome definitions [14]. When the evidence base consists entirely of placebo-controlled trials, the choice of which trials to include and how to define outcomes becomes a decision that can flip the conclusion.

## The transitivity problem in practice

The transitivity assumption is not merely a statistical nicety; it has concrete clinical content. The SGLT2 inhibitor trials and GLP-1 receptor agonist trials enrolled different kinds of patients. The SGLT2 inhibitor node included dedicated renal outcome trials that enrolled patients with substantially impaired kidney function, while the GLP-1 receptor agonist node drew on cardiovascular outcome trials in which the proportion of patients with CKD was relatively low [1][4]. A real-world analysis found that only 0.9% of a primary care CKD population would have been eligible for CREDENCE, 2.2% for DAPA-CKD, and 8.0% for EMPA-KIDNEY [6]. The trials that anchor the SGLT2 inhibitor node are therefore not representative of the broader CKD population, and the indirect comparison inherits that limitation.

The FLOW trial, the first dedicated kidney outcome trial of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, partially redressed this imbalance. Semaglutide significantly reduced the kidney-specific component of the primary outcome and slowed the annual rate of eGFR decline, with effects observed across prespecified subgroups including participants on concurrent SGLT2 inhibitors (P for interaction = 0.109) [14]. But FLOW is itself placebo-controlled, so it does not provide a direct comparison with SGLT2 inhibitors; it merely adds a second, parallel placebo-anchored arm to the network. The finding that semaglutide's kidney benefits were consistent regardless of concurrent SGLT2 inhibitor use is suggestive of additive effects, but it is an observation within a placebo-controlled trial, not a head-to-head comparison [14].

## The consequence for the report's premise

The practical consequence is that every comparative statement about SGLT2 inhibitors versus GLP-1 receptor agonists in type 2 diabetes with CKD is an indirect estimate, and indirect estimates are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. A 2026 network meta-analysis in patients with type 2 diabetes and prior myocardial infarction put it directly: "In the absence of head-to-head trials, all comparative findings are hypothesis-generating. Treatment selection should be individualized" [12]. The same logic applies with greater force in CKD, where the trial populations are even more heterogeneous.

The absence of head-to-head evidence also explains why guidelines have moved cautiously. After FLOW, recent guidelines elevated GLP-1 receptor agonists to the same level of recommendation as SGLT2 inhibitors for people with CKD [14]. That elevation reflects the strength of each class against placebo, not any demonstrated superiority of one over the other. The SMART-C collaborative meta-analysis, which examined the efficacy and safety of SGLT2 inhibitors with and without GLP-1 receptor agonists, found additive benefits of combination therapy in observational analyses, but the authors called for large-scale randomized trials evaluating the combination [4][14].

For the purposes of this report, the implication is decisive. Any comparison of efficacy and adverse event profiles between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in type 2 diabetes with concurrent CKD must be understood as an indirect comparison resting on the transitivity assumption, with all the limitations that entails. The sample sizes and p-values extracted from the most cited trials — CREDENCE, DAPA-CKD, and FLOW — are each estimates of effect against placebo, not against the competing class. The premise that a direct comparison can be drawn from the randomized evidence fails; what remains is a network of placebo-controlled trials whose indirect comparisons are informative but not definitive.

## Sample Sizes and Primary Efficacy P-Values of the Landmark Kidney Trials

### The Three Landmark Trials at a Glance

The three SGLT2 inhibitor kidney-outcome trials that anchor this literature — CREDENCE (canagliflozin, 2019), DAPA-CKD (dapagliflozin, 2020), and EMPA-KIDNEY (empagliflozin, 2022) — enrolled 4,401, 4,304, and 6,609 participants respectively, and each reported a statistically significant reduction in its primary composite efficacy outcome against placebo. The headline figures are: CREDENCE P=0.00001 (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.59–0.82) [2][28]; DAPA-CKD P<0.001 (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.51–0.72) [27]; and EMPA-KIDNEY P<0.001 (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.64–0.82) [2]. These numbers are frequently quoted in comparative reviews, but their meaning depends on three qualifications that are often glossed over: the trials compared each drug against placebo, not against each other; the primary composite endpoints were defined differently across the three studies; and the enrolled populations differed substantially in baseline kidney function and diabetes prevalence. Each of these points is developed below.

### Sample Sizes: Enrollment and What the Numbers Represent

The total enrolled populations are consistently reported across independent sources. CREDENCE randomized 4,401 participants with type 2 diabetes and CKD, a figure stated directly in a review of kidney outcome trials [14] and confirmed in the trial's results announcement, which notes that "4401 patients had undergone randomization" at the time the trial was stopped [28]. The network meta-analysis by the PMC12781563 authors lists CREDENCE's sample size as 4,401 in its trial characteristics table [4]. DAPA-CKD enrolled 4,304 participants, of whom 67.5% had type 2 diabetes at baseline [14]; the trial publication confirms this total by reporting 2,152 participants randomized to dapagliflozin and 2,152 to placebo [27]. EMPA-KIDNEY enrolled 6,609 participants, with 46% having diabetes (96.4% of those type 2) [14]; the American College of Cardiology trial summary confirms "Total number of enrollees: 6,609," split 3,304 to empagliflozin and 3,305 to placebo [25].

A discrepancy worth flagging appears in a real-world generalizability study that compares trial participants with primary care CKD populations. Its Table 3 labels a "Trial cohort (N = 2202)" for CREDENCE and its Table 2 labels "Trial cohort (N = 2152)" for DAPA-CKD [6]. These are not alternative enrollment totals; they are the active-treatment arm sizes (approximately half of each trial's total). A reader consulting only these tables could mistake 2,202 or 2,152 for the full trial population. The same source's text elsewhere confirms the full-trial framing, reporting eligibility proportions against the complete trial cohorts [6]. The lesson is that per-arm and total figures coexist in the literature, and any comparison across trials must use the same basis.

A second qualification concerns what the totals include. DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY deliberately enrolled participants with and without diabetes — 32.5% of DAPA-CKD and 54% of EMPA-KIDNEY participants did not have diabetes [14]. CREDENCE, by contrast, required type 2 diabetes for entry [14]. The network meta-analysis that pools these trials notes this explicitly: for trials enrolling mixed populations, the "Total N" represents the full trial population, but data extraction for the meta-analysis was restricted to the type 2 diabetes subgroup where reported [4]. This matters for the present report because the question concerns patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD; the full-trial p-values reported below therefore include non-diabetic participants in DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY, and the effect estimates in the diabetic subgroup are slightly different from the overall results. For DAPA-CKD, for instance, the primary outcome was reduced by 36% in the type 2 diabetes subgroup (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.52–0.79) versus 39% overall [2].

### Primary Composite Outcomes and Their P-Values

The three trials did not share a single primary endpoint, which complicates direct comparison of their p-values.

**CREDENCE** used a composite of end-stage kidney disease, doubling of serum creatinine, or death from renal or cardiovascular causes [2][28]. The trial was stopped early after a prespecified interim analysis showed benefit; at adjudication, the relative risk of the primary outcome was 30% lower with canagliflozin, with event rates of 43.2 versus 61.2 per 1,000 patient-years (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.59–0.82, P=0.00001) [28]. The same p-value is reproduced in a narrative review of SGLT2 inhibitors in type 2 diabetes and CKD [2]. The unusually precise p-value (0.00001 rather than the more common <0.001) reflects the trial's early stopping and the large separation between arms; it is reported identically in both the ABCD announcement of the NEJM publication and the later review [2][28].

**DAPA-CKD** used a composite of sustained ≥50% decline in eGFR, end-stage kidney disease, or death from renal or cardiovascular causes [27]. The independent data monitoring committee recommended stopping the trial for efficacy [27]. Over a median of 2.4 years, a primary outcome event occurred in 197 of 2,152 dapagliflozin participants (9.2%) versus 312 of 2,152 placebo participants (14.5%), yielding HR 0.61 (95% CI 0.51–0.72, P<0.001) [27]. The number needed to treat to prevent one primary event was 19 (95% CI 15–27) [27]. The p-value is reported as <0.001 in the trial publication [27] and as a 39% relative risk reduction in the review [14].

**EMPA-KIDNEY** used a composite of kidney disease progression or cardiovascular death [2][14]. The trial was also stopped early after a prespecified interim review identified unequivocal efficacy [2]. At adjudication, empagliflozin reduced the primary outcome with HR 0.72 (95% CI 0.64–0.82, P<0.001) [2]. The review describes this as a 28% reduction relative to placebo [14]. The ACC summary confirms the design and enrollment but does not restate the primary p-value; the narrative review provides it [2][25].

All three p-values are therefore well below conventional significance thresholds, but they are not directly comparable as measures of relative efficacy because the endpoints, follow-up durations, and baseline risk profiles differ. CREDENCE enrolled participants with a mean eGFR of 56.2 mL/min/1.73 m² and median UACR of 927 mg/g; DAPA-CKD enrolled participants with mean eGFR 43.1 and median UACR 949 mg/g; EMPA-KIDNEY enrolled participants with mean eGFR 37.3 and median UACR 329 mg/g [14]. The trials thus spanned a range of CKD severity, with EMPA-KIDNEY capturing the most advanced kidney impairment and the lowest albuminuria.

### Placebo-Controlled, Not Head-to-Head

A critical clarification for any reader of this literature: CREDENCE, DAPA-CKD, and EMPA-KIDNEY each compared the active drug against placebo, not against one another or against a GLP-1 receptor agonist. The network meta-analysis that synthesizes these trials states its inclusion criterion plainly — "randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials" [4] — and the review of diabetic kidney disease trials notes that "direct head-to-head trials are lacking" [14]. No randomized trial directly comparing an SGLT2 inhibitor with a GLP-1 receptor agonist in type 2 diabetes with CKD has been identified; a 2021 systematic review found none, and a network meta-analysis published after a December 2025 search still reported no head-to-head trials [4][14].

The practical consequence is that all comparisons between the two drug classes are indirect, derived from network meta-analyses that connect them through the placebo arms. The most recent such analysis, pooling 14 trials and 117,633 participants, found no statistically significant difference between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists for MACE (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.94–1.13) but found SGLT2 inhibitors superior for hospitalization for heart failure (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.66–0.85) and composite renal outcomes (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.66–0.87) [4]. A separate CKD-focused network meta-analysis of 29 studies reached a similar conclusion, reporting that SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with significantly lower risks of composite renal outcomes compared with GLP-1 receptor agonists (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.64–0.91, P=0.003) [11]. These indirect estimates are the best available evidence for class comparison, but they rest on the assumption that the placebo arms of the constituent trials are exchangeable — an assumption the network meta-analysis itself flags as imperfect, since SGLT2 inhibitor trials enrolled patients with lower baseline eGFR (weighted mean 65.2 vs 76.8 mL/min/1.73 m²) and a higher proportion with heart failure (22.4% vs 14.5%) than GLP-1 receptor agonist trials [4].

The p-values reported for the three landmark trials are therefore p-values for drug-versus-placebo comparisons. They establish that each drug works in this population; they do not establish which drug works better. That question is answered only by the indirect network meta-analyses, with their attendant uncertainty.

### Discrepancies and Caveats in Reported Figures

Several discrepancies and caveats deserve explicit mention.

First, the per-arm versus total confusion noted above: source [6] reports trial cohort sizes of 2,202 (CREDENCE) and 2,152 (DAPA-CKD) in its comparison tables, which are the active-treatment arms, not the totals. The same source's eligibility analyses use the full trial populations [6]. Anyone extracting sample sizes from secondary tables should verify whether the figure is per-arm or total.

Second, the p-value for CREDENCE comes from a source affiliated with the Association of British Clinical Diabetologists (ABCD), which reproduced the NEJM publication's results [28], and is corroborated by an independent narrative review [2]. The DAPA-CKD p-value comes from the trial publication itself as indexed in a university research portal [27]. The EMPA-KIDNEY p-value comes from a narrative review [2]; the ACC summary confirms the design and the non-significance of certain secondary endpoints but does not restate the primary p-value [25]. The primary NEJM publication for EMPA-KIDNEY would be the definitive source, but it is not among the material provided.

Third, all three trials were stopped early for benefit — CREDENCE after a prespecified interim analysis [28], DAPA-CKD on the recommendation of the independent data monitoring committee [27], and EMPA-KIDNEY after a prespecified interim review [2]. Early stopping can inflate observed effect sizes, and the p-values should be interpreted with that in mind, though the magnitude of the effects and their consistency across trials mitigate this concern.

Fourth, the p-values apply to the full enrolled populations, which for DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY include non-diabetic participants. For the type 2 diabetes subgroup specifically, DAPA-CKD's primary outcome was reduced by 36% (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.52–0.79) [2], slightly less than the 39% overall reduction [14]. The EMPA-KIDNEY type 2 diabetes subgroup data were extracted from subgroup analyses in the primary publication for the network meta-analysis [4], but the subgroup-specific p-value is not stated in the provided sources.

Finally, the trials' baseline characteristics differ in ways that bear on generalizability. The real-world comparison study found that trial participants had lower eGFR and higher albuminuria than the primary care CKD populations who would have been eligible for the trials, and that women were under-represented in all three trials [6]. These differences do not affect the internal validity of the p-values, but they qualify how confidently the results extend to the broader type 2 diabetes and CKD population.

### What Follows from These Figures

The sample sizes and p-values establish three things. First, each of the three SGLT2 inhibitors tested — canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, and empagliflozin — reduces the risk of its primary composite kidney outcome in patients with CKD, with or without diabetes, at conventional levels of statistical significance. Second, the trials were powered adequately to detect these effects: the smallest, DAPA-CKD, had 4,304 participants and still produced a highly significant result with a narrow confidence interval (0.51–0.72) [27]. Third, because all three trials were placebo-controlled, the p-values cannot be read as evidence of superiority of one SGLT2 inhibitor over another, nor of the class over GLP-1 receptor agonists. The comparative question is answered only through indirect network meta-analyses, which currently favor SGLT2 inhibitors for renal composite outcomes and heart failure hospitalization, with no significant difference for MACE [4][11]. Any report that cites these three trials as "comparing SGLT2 inhibitors with GLP-1 receptor agonists" is mischaracterizing their design; they are the placebo-controlled foundation on which indirect comparisons are built, not direct comparisons themselves.

## Secondary Efficacy Outcomes: P-Values Across the Three Trials

### The Structure of Secondary Outcome Reporting Across the Three Trials

The three landmark SGLT2 inhibitor kidney-outcome trials — CREDENCE (canagliflozin, 2019), DAPA-CKD (dapagliflozin, 2020), and EMPA-KIDNEY (empagliflozin, 2022) — each pre-specified a hierarchy of secondary efficacy outcomes, and the p-values attached to those outcomes must be read in the context of the hierarchical testing procedures each trial employed. In CREDENCE, the secondary outcomes were tested in a fixed sequence: the composite of cardiovascular (CV) death or hospitalization for heart failure (HHF), then the composite of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE; CV death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, or non-fatal stroke), then HHF alone, then the renal-specific composite of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), doubling of serum creatinine, or renal death, then CV death, then all-cause mortality, and finally a broader CV composite [17]. Because statistical significance was required before proceeding to the next hypothesis in the hierarchy, the p-values reported for outcomes later in the sequence carry a different evidentiary weight than those reported earlier [17]. This procedural detail matters for interpreting the CREDENCE results: the p-value for CV death was 0.0502, which did not meet the significance threshold, and consequently the remaining secondary outcomes (all-cause mortality and the broader CV composite) were not formally tested [17].

DAPA-CKD likewise pre-specified a hierarchical testing order, and the trial's secondary outcomes were reported with p-values that reflect the same logic [17]. EMPA-KIDNEY pre-specified key secondary confirmatory endpoints including all-cause hospitalization, hospitalization for heart failure or CV death, and all-cause mortality [26]. The three trials also differed in their primary composite definitions, which affects how their secondary outcomes relate to the primary result: CREDENCE's primary composite was ESKD, doubling of serum creatinine, or renal/CV death [2]; DAPA-CKD's was sustained ≥50% eGFR decline, ESKD, or renal/CV death [27]; and EMPA-KIDNEY's was kidney disease progression (≥40% eGFR decline, ESKD, or renal death) or CV death [25].

### CREDENCE: Secondary Outcomes and Their P-Values

CREDENCE enrolled 4,401 participants with type 2 diabetes and CKD (mean eGFR 56.2 mL/min/1.73 m², median UACR 927 mg/g) [14]. The secondary outcomes reported with p-values were:

- **End-stage kidney disease**: HR 0.68 (95% CI 0.54–0.86), P=0.002 [28][2]
- **CV death, myocardial infarction, or stroke (MACE)**: HR 0.80 (95% CI 0.67–0.95), P=0.01 [28][17]
- **Hospitalization for heart failure**: HR 0.61 (95% CI 0.47–0.80), P<0.001 [28][17]
- **Composite of CV death and HHF**: HR 0.69 (95% CI 0.57–0.83), P<0.001 [17]
- **Renal-specific composite (ESKD, doubling of serum creatinine, or renal death)**: HR 0.66 (95% CI 0.53–0.81), P<0.001 [2][17]
- **CV death**: P=0.0502, not statistically significant [17]

The ESKD p-value of 0.002 corresponds to a 32% relative risk reduction [2][28]. The MACE p-value of 0.01 corresponds to a 20% relative risk reduction [17]. The HHF p-value of <0.001 corresponds to a 39% relative risk reduction [17]. The renal-specific composite p-value of <0.001 corresponds to a 34% relative risk reduction [2][17].

The CREDENCE p-values come from the original NEJM publication (April 14, 2019) as reported by the Association of British Clinical Diabetologists [28] and from a Janssen product page [17]. The Janssen page is industry-affiliated (Janssen markets canagliflozin as INVOKANA), which is a consideration for source weighting, though its figures are consistent with the ABCD announcement and the narrative review [2].

### DAPA-CKD: Secondary Outcomes and Their P-Values

DAPA-CKD enrolled 4,304 participants (67.5% with type 2 diabetes), with mean eGFR 43.1 mL/min/1.73 m² and median UACR 949 mg/g [14]. The secondary outcomes reported with p-values were:

- **Renal composite (sustained ≥50% eGFR decline, ESKD, or renal death)**: HR 0.56 (95% CI 0.45–0.68), P<0.001 [27][17]
- **Composite of CV death or HHF**: HR 0.71 (95% CI 0.55–0.92), P=0.009 [27][17]
- **All-cause death**: HR 0.69 (95% CI 0.53–0.88), P=0.004 [27][17]

The renal composite p-value of <0.001 corresponds to a 44% relative risk reduction [14]. The all-cause death p-value of 0.004 corresponds to a 31% relative risk reduction, with 101 deaths (4.7%) in the dapagliflozin group versus 146 (6.8%) in placebo [27]. The DAPA-CKD p-values come from the trial's primary publication as reported in a research portal entry [27] and from the Janssen comparison page [17]. Both sources report identical figures, which increases confidence in their accuracy. Notably, the DAPA-CKD secondary renal composite differs from the primary composite by excluding CV death — the primary composite included death from renal or cardiovascular causes, while the secondary renal composite included only renal death [27]. This distinction matters because the secondary composite isolates the kidney-specific effect.

### EMPA-KIDNEY: Secondary Outcomes and Their P-Values

EMPA-KIDNEY enrolled 6,609 participants with CKD (46% with diabetes), with mean eGFR 37.3 mL/min/1.73 m² [14][25]. The secondary outcomes reported with p-values were:

- **All-cause hospitalization**: HR 0.86 (95% CI 0.78–0.95), p=0.0025 [26] (reported as p=0.003 in the ACC summary [25] and the narrative review [2])
- **Hospitalization for heart failure or CV death**: 4.0% vs 4.6%, p>0.05 (not statistically significant) [25]
- **All-cause mortality**: 4.5% vs 5.1%, p>0.05 (not statistically significant) [25]

The all-cause hospitalization p-value of 0.0025 corresponds to a 14% relative risk reduction [26]. The sponsor's press release (Boehringer Ingelheim) notes that the reductions in HHF or CV death and all-cause death were not statistically significant, with limited power due to the number of events observed [26]. The discrepancy between p=0.0025 (PRNewswire) and p=0.003 (ACC and narrative review) for all-cause hospitalization likely reflects rounding in the secondary sources; the underlying HR and 95% CI are identical (0.86, 0.78–0.95) across all three sources [26][25][2].

### Gaps Where P-Values Are Missing

Several p-values are not available from the provided sources:

1. **EMPA-KIDNEY**: Exact p-values for the non-significant secondary endpoints (HHF or CV death; all-cause mortality) are not reported — only "p>0.05" is given [25]. The sponsor's press release similarly states these endpoints were "not statistically significant" without providing exact values [26]. The long-term follow-up data from the ACC summary report a CV death HR of 0.75 (95% CI 0.59–0.95) without a p-value [25].

2. **DAPA-CKD**: Separate p-values for CV death alone and HHF alone are not provided; only the composite of CV death or HHF (p=0.009) is reported [27][17].

3. **CREDENCE**: The p-value for CV death (0.0502) is reported, but because it did not reach significance, the subsequent outcomes (all-cause mortality and the broader CV composite) were not formally tested and no p-values are reported for them [17].

4. **Adverse event outcomes**: No p-values are reported for serious adverse events, hypoglycemia, or genital infections in any of the three trials in the provided sources [7][4][2].

### Source Quality and Hierarchical Testing as Qualifications

The p-values for CREDENCE come from three sources: the ABCD announcement [28], the Janssen product page [17], and a narrative review [2]. The Janssen page is industry-affiliated, which warrants caution, though its figures are consistent with the other sources. The DAPA-CKD p-values come from a research portal entry [27] and the same Janssen page [17]. The EMPA-KIDNEY p-values come from a Boehringer Ingelheim press release [26] and the American College of Cardiology summary [25]; the press release is industry-affiliated (Boehringer Ingelheim co-markets empagliflozin as Jardiance). The narrative review [2] provides independent confirmation of the CREDENCE and EMPA-KIDNEY figures.

The hierarchical testing procedures are important for interpretation. In CREDENCE, the fact that CV death did not reach significance (P=0.0502) meant that all-cause mortality was never formally tested [17]. This is a critical qualification: the absence of a reported p-value for all-cause mortality in CREDENCE does not mean the outcome was neutral; it means the trial's pre-specified testing procedure did not permit formal inference. Similarly, in EMPA-KIDNEY, the non-significant results for HHF or CV death and all-cause mortality were attributed by the sponsor to limited power from low event numbers, not necessarily to a true absence of effect [26].

### What the Pattern of P-Values Implies

The pattern of p-values across the three trials is consistent with the broader evidence base for SGLT2 inhibitors. The strongest and most consistent signals are for renal outcomes (ESKD in CREDENCE p=0.002; renal composite in DAPA-CKD p<0.001) and heart failure hospitalization (CREDENCE p<0.001; DAPA-CKD composite p=0.009). All-cause mortality was significantly reduced in DAPA-CKD (p=0.004) but not in EMPA-KIDNEY (p>0.05), and not formally tested in CREDENCE. This pattern aligns with the network meta-analysis finding that SGLT2 inhibitors are particularly effective for heart failure hospitalization (HR 0.75 vs GLP-1RAs) [4]. The renal composite p-values are the most robust across trials, consistent with the class-level finding that SGLT2 inhibitors reduce composite renal outcomes more than GLP-1 receptor agonists (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.66–0.87 for SGLT2i vs GLP-1RA) [4]. However, because these three trials are placebo-controlled rather than head-to-head comparisons, the p-values describe each drug's effect against placebo, not against a GLP-1 receptor agonist; any between-class inference must come from indirect network meta-analyses [4][14].

## Renal Outcomes: Indirect Comparison Favoring SGLT2 Inhibitors

### The Network Meta-Analysis Evidence Base

The central quantitative claim in this chapter rests on a 2026 systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) that pooled 14 large-scale randomized controlled trials involving 117,633 participants with type 2 diabetes, comparing SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists against placebo [4]. In the indirect network comparison, SGLT2 inhibitors were significantly superior to GLP-1 receptor agonists in preventing a composite renal outcome — defined as sustained eGFR decline, end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), or renal death — with a hazard ratio of 0.76 (95% CI 0.66–0.87) [4]. The same analysis ranked SGLT2 inhibitors as the most effective treatment for renal protection, with a P-score of 0.99 on a scale where 1.0 represents the highest probability of being the best among compared treatments [4]. The authors describe this as a 24% relative risk reduction for SGLT2 inhibitors over GLP-1 receptor agonists on the composite renal outcome [4].

The magnitude of this between-class difference is best appreciated against the placebo-referenced effects. In the pairwise meta-analyses, SGLT2 inhibitors reduced the composite renal outcome by 38% relative to placebo (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.56–0.70), whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists achieved an 18% reduction (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.75–0.89) [4]. Both classes therefore confer genuine renal protection, but the effect size for SGLT2 inhibitors is roughly double that of GLP-1 receptor agonists, and the 95% confidence intervals of the two placebo comparisons do not overlap — a pattern consistent with the significant indirect comparison between the classes [4]. The 2026 NMA rated the certainty of evidence for the comparative composite renal outcome as High, citing the strong signal and consistency across dedicated renal trials [4]. This stands in contrast to the Moderate rating assigned to the comparative MACE outcome, which was downgraded for indirectness due to the absence of head-to-head trials [4].

### Corroborating Network Meta-Analyses

The 2026 NMA is not an isolated finding. A CKD-focused systematic review and NMA published in 2021, restricted to patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease, identified 13 studies with 32,949 patients and reported that SGLT2 inhibitors reduced a composite of renal outcomes with a risk ratio of 0.68 (95% CI 0.59–0.78), whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists did not reach statistical significance for the same outcome (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.72–1.03) [3]. This analysis is notable because it was conducted in the specific population of interest — type 2 diabetes with CKD — rather than the broader type 2 diabetes population of the 2026 NMA, and it found that GLP-1 receptor agonists failed to demonstrate a significant renal benefit at all in this higher-risk group [3].

A 2023 NMA of 29 studies (50,938 participants for MACE and 49,965 for composite renal outcomes) in patients with CKD and type 2 diabetes similarly found SGLT2 inhibitors associated with significantly lower risks of composite renal outcomes compared with GLP-1 receptor agonists (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.64–0.91; P = 0.003), concluding that SGLT2 inhibitors provide better cardiorenal protection than GLP-1 receptor agonists in this population [11]. This analysis also included a third comparator — non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists — and found SGLT2 inhibitors superior to that class as well (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.68–0.90; P = 0.001), further positioning SGLT2 inhibitors as the leading renal-protective agent among the three drug classes [11].

Not all syntheses agree on every endpoint. A comprehensive NMA of 31 trials involving 98,234 participants with type 2 diabetes found that both drug classes significantly reduced the risk of kidney failure (defined as eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m² or renal replacement therapy) compared with placebo, but reported no significant differences between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in absolute risk reductions for kidney failure [14]. The discrepancy between this finding and the significant between-class differences in the 2026 and 2023 analyses likely reflects differences in outcome definition — the 31-trial analysis used a narrow kidney-failure endpoint, whereas the analyses showing between-class differences used broader composites that include sustained eGFR decline and renal death, which accumulate more events and therefore have greater statistical power to detect differences [14][4].

### Composite Definitions Vary Across Trials

A critical qualification when interpreting these pooled estimates is that the component trials used materially different composite definitions. The four landmark kidney-outcome trials illustrate the variation. CREDENCE (canagliflozin, 2019) used a primary composite of ESKD, doubling of serum creatinine, or renal or cardiovascular death, reporting a hazard ratio of 0.70 (95% CI 0.59–0.82) [14][2]. DAPA-CKD (dapagliflozin, 2020) used a composite of sustained eGFR decline ≥50%, ESKD, or renal or cardiovascular death, with HR 0.61 (95% CI 0.51–0.72) [14]. EMPA-KIDNEY (empagliflozin, 2022) defined its primary outcome as progression of kidney disease (ESKD, sustained eGFR <10 mL/min/1.73 m², sustained eGFR decline ≥40%, or renal death) or cardiovascular death, with HR 0.72 (95% CI 0.64–0.82) [14]. FLOW (semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 2024) used a composite of kidney failure, sustained eGFR decline ≥50%, or kidney-related or cardiovascular death, with HR 0.76 (95% CI 0.66–0.88) [14]. The number needed to treat for the primary composite was 22 (95% CI 15–38) in CREDENCE, 19 (95% CI 15–27) in DAPA-CKD, 28 (95% CI 19–53) in EMPA-KIDNEY, and 20 (95% CI 14–40) in FLOW [14].

The 2026 NMA explicitly acknowledges that the composite renal outcome definitions varied slightly across the 13 trials contributing data, citing the 40% versus 50% eGFR decline threshold as an example [4]. This variation matters for two reasons. First, a 40% eGFR decline threshold captures events earlier and more frequently than a 50% threshold, so trials using the looser definition contribute more events and can shift pooled estimates. Second, the inclusion or exclusion of cardiovascular death in the renal composite — present in CREDENCE, DAPA-CKD, and EMPA-KIDNEY but not in the kidney-specific secondary composites of some trials — means the "renal outcome" being compared is not identical across studies [14]. A meta-analysis that standardized the outcome definition across all 13 placebo-controlled SGLT2 inhibitor trials, using a sustained ≥50% eGFR decline, ESKD, or death from kidney disease, found a 37% relative risk reduction (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.58–0.69) across over 90,000 participants — a result consistent with the 2026 NMA's placebo comparison and reassuring that the definitional heterogeneity does not materially distort the class-level estimate [2].

### The Absence of Direct Head-to-Head Evidence

The most important structural limitation of this evidence base is that no randomized controlled trial has directly compared SGLT2 inhibitors with GLP-1 receptor agonists for kidney outcomes in diabetic kidney disease [14]. A 2021 systematic review stated that no study to date had compared the two classes in this population, and the 2026 NMA still reported that no head-to-head trials were included [3][4]. The network geometry is therefore star-shaped, with placebo as the central node and both drug classes connected only to placebo [12]. This has two consequences. First, all between-class comparisons are indirect, derived from the relative effects of each class against placebo, and therefore depend on the transitivity assumption — that the placebo arms across trials are exchangeable. Second, formal inconsistency testing is impossible in a star-shaped network, so the validity of the indirect comparison cannot be statistically verified [12]. The 2026 NMA did perform a global inconsistency test for the primary MACE outcome (Q = 3.54, df = 2, p = 0.17), which did not reveal significant inconsistency, but for the secondary outcome of hospitalization for heart failure the test indicated potential inconsistency (Q = 6.82, df = 2, p = 0.03), driven by the varying magnitudes of benefit observed in dedicated heart failure trials compared to general diabetes trials [4]. For the renal composite, no direct evidence was available, so the consistency check relied entirely on agreement between indirect estimates derived from placebo-controlled trials [4].

The transitivity assumption is strained by systematic differences in trial populations. In the 2026 NMA, SGLT2 inhibitor trials actively recruited patients with heart failure (22.4% with a history of heart failure), whereas GLP-1 receptor agonist trials often excluded severe heart failure (NYHA class III–IV), and the between-group difference in heart failure history was flagged as dissimilar [4]. Since heart failure status is itself a risk factor for renal decline, this imbalance could bias the indirect comparison, although the direction of any bias is uncertain. The 2026 NMA rated the certainty of evidence for the comparative MACE outcome as Moderate, downgraded for indirectness due to the absence of head-to-head trials, while rating the evidence for SGLT2 inhibitor benefit on heart failure hospitalization as High [4].

Observational studies provide a partial, non-randomized check on the indirect estimates, with mixed results. A large PCORnet cohort study found SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists showed comparable effectiveness for a composite kidney outcome including sustained ≥40% eGFR decline, ESKD, or mortality [14]. A target trial emulation using U.S. healthcare databases similarly found no significant difference between the classes in a composite of >50% eGFR decline, ESKD, or all-cause mortality [14]. However, a multicenter retrospective cohort study including a higher-risk population (23% with CKD) reported slower eGFR decline and lower risks of CKD progression and doubling of serum creatinine among SGLT2 inhibitor users compared with GLP-1 receptor agonist users [14]. A comparative effectiveness study using inverse probability of treatment weighting in patients with type 2 diabetes and mild/moderate CKD found SGLT2 inhibitor use associated with a significantly lower risk of a composite of death, myocardial infarction, or stroke (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.61–0.99) and of non-fatal myocardial infarction (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.53–0.98) relative to GLP-1 receptor agonists [1]. The authors of the review note that baseline eGFR and albuminuria values in the null observational studies suggest participants were not predominantly at high risk for CKD progression, whereas the studies favoring SGLT2 inhibitors included higher-risk populations — a pattern consistent with the hypothesis that the between-class difference in renal outcomes is most detectable where event rates are highest [14].

### What Explains the Difference

The mechanisms proposed to explain the differential renal effects are distinct. GLP-1 receptor agonists are thought to act through anti-atherogenic and anti-inflammatory pathways, whereas SGLT2 inhibitors provide advantage through hemodynamic effects — specifically, reducing intraglomerular pressure by restoring tubuloglomerular feedback — and enhanced cardiac energetics [4]. The eGFR slope data from the landmark trials are consistent with a hemodynamic mechanism: the between-group difference in eGFR slope (mL/min/1.73 m² per year) was 1.52 (95% CI 1.11–1.93) in CREDENCE, 0.93 (95% CI 0.61–1.25) in DAPA-CKD, 0.75 (95% CI 0.54–0.96) in EMPA-KIDNEY, and 1.16 (95% CI 0.86–1.47) in FLOW [14]. The between-group difference in UACR reduction was 31% (95% CI 26–35) in CREDENCE, 29% (95% CI 25–33) in DAPA-CKD, 19% in EMPA-KIDNEY, and a smaller reduction in FLOW [14]. The review notes that SGLT2 inhibitors appear more effective in mitigating glomerular hyperfiltration, whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists are particularly beneficial in reducing albuminuria and atherosclerotic events [14].

### Qualifications and Caveats

Several qualifications temper the interpretation of the HR 0.76 indirect estimate. First, the estimate is an indirect comparison, and although the 2026 NMA rated the renal outcome evidence as High, the comparative MACE evidence was downgraded for indirectness [4]. Second, the composite definitions vary across trials, as detailed above, and the 2026 NMA pooled trials using both 40% and 50% eGFR decline thresholds [4]. Third, the observational evidence is genuinely mixed, with null findings in lower-risk populations and positive findings favoring SGLT2 inhibitors in higher-risk populations [14][1]. Fourth, the 31-trial NMA found no significant between-class difference for the narrow kidney-failure endpoint, suggesting the advantage of SGLT2 inhibitors may be concentrated in the eGFR-decline and renal-death components of the composite rather than in progression to kidney failure itself [14]. Fifth, a 2025 NMA suggested that while SGLT2 inhibitors reduce renal events in patients with moderate renal impairment, GLP-1 receptor agonists may be more effective in reducing all-cause mortality in patients with preserved eGFR (>90 mL/min/1.73 m²), indicating that the relative benefits may vary by baseline kidney function [4]. Finally, the 2026 NMA notes that the renal benefit of SGLT2 inhibitors appears consistent irrespective of diabetes status, kidney function, or the primary cause of kidney disease, which supports a class effect but also means the estimates are not specific to the type 2 diabetes with CKD population [2][4].

Taken together, the network meta-analytic evidence consistently favors SGLT2 inhibitors over GLP-1 receptor agonists for composite renal outcomes in type 2 diabetes, with a between-class hazard ratio of 0.76 (95% CI 0.66–0.87) from the largest and most recent synthesis [4], corroborated by CKD-specific analyses reporting risk ratios of 0.68 and 0.77 [3][11]. Both classes reduce renal risk relative to placebo, but the SGLT2 inhibitor effect is substantially larger [4]. The absence of direct head-to-head randomized data, the variation in composite definitions, and the mixed observational record all argue for interpreting the indirect estimate as the best available — but not definitive — evidence of a class-level difference in renal protection.

## Cardiovascular Outcomes: MACE Similar, Heart Failure Hospitalization Favors SGLT2i

The comparative cardiovascular efficacy of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease rests entirely on indirect evidence. No head-to-head randomized controlled trial has directly compared the two classes, and the evidence base is therefore derived from network meta-analyses that anchor both classes against placebo-controlled cardiovascular outcome trials [4]. This limitation shapes every estimate reported below and is reflected in the certainty grading assigned to the findings.

## Major adverse cardiovascular events: no significant difference

Across 14 trials enrolling 117,633 participants, both drug classes significantly reduced the risk of MACE relative to placebo: SGLT2 inhibitors by 11% (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.84–0.94) and GLP-1 receptor agonists by 14% (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.93) [4]. The indirect comparison between the two classes yielded a hazard ratio of 1.03 (95% CI 0.94–1.13), indicating no statistically significant difference in MACE reduction [4]. The point estimate slightly favors GLP-1 receptor agonists, and the ranking analysis reflects this: GLP-1 receptor agonists achieved a P-score of 0.83 versus 0.67 for SGLT2 inhibitors, meaning GLP-1 receptor agonists had a higher probability of being the most effective treatment for MACE among the options compared [4]. However, the confidence interval crosses unity, and the GRADE assessment rated the certainty of this comparison as moderate, downgraded once for indirectness because no head-to-head trials exist [4].

Sensitivity analyses reinforce the robustness of the MACE finding. Excluding trials with follow-up shorter than 1.5 years produced an HR of 1.03 (95% CI 0.96–1.10), and excluding sotagliflozin—a dual SGLT1/2 inhibitor—yielded an HR of 1.02 (95% CI 0.93–1.12), both consistent with the primary analysis [4]. The network showed no significant inconsistency for MACE (Q = 3.54, df = 2, p = 0.17) [4].

The CKD-specific evidence adds an important nuance. A network meta-analysis restricted to patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease, pooling 13 studies with 32,949 patients, found that SGLT2 inhibitors significantly reduced MACE relative to placebo (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.75–0.96), whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists did not (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.80–1.04) [3]. The indirect comparison between the classes showed no significant difference (RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.78–1.12), consistent with the broader analysis, but the failure of GLP-1 receptor agonists to separate from placebo in this CKD population is notable [3]. A sensitivity analysis within that same study revealed heterogeneity within the GLP-1 receptor agonist class: GLP-1 analogues (e.g., liraglutide, semaglutide) significantly reduced MACE versus placebo (RR 0.81, 95% CI 0.69–0.95), whereas exendin-4-based agents (e.g., exenatide, lixisenatide) did not (RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.88–1.20) [3]. This suggests that class-level pooling may obscure meaningful differences between individual GLP-1 receptor agonists in the CKD population.

A second CKD-focused network meta-analysis, including 29 studies with 50,938 participants for MACE, similarly found that SGLT2 inhibitors did not significantly reduce MACE compared with GLP-1 receptor agonists [11]. The consistency of this null finding across multiple independent network meta-analyses—both general-population and CKD-restricted—supports the conclusion that the two classes are broadly comparable for atherosclerotic event prevention, while leaving open the possibility of class-specific differences in specific subgroups.

## Hospitalization for heart failure: significant advantage for SGLT2 inhibitors

The clearest cardiovascular divergence between the classes emerges for hospitalization for heart failure. In the pairwise meta-analysis, SGLT2 inhibitors reduced HHF risk by 32% relative to placebo (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.63–0.73), whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists produced a modest 9% reduction (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.84–0.99) [4]. The indirect comparison between the classes yielded an HR of 0.75 (95% CI 0.66–0.85), a statistically significant 25% relative risk reduction favoring SGLT2 inhibitors [4]. SGLT2 inhibitors ranked as the most effective treatment for HHF prevention with a P-score of 0.99, compared with 0.51 for GLP-1 receptor agonists [4]. The GRADE assessment rated this comparison as high certainty, reflecting the large effect size and consistency across sensitivity analyses [4].

The mechanistic basis for this divergence is well established. SGLT2 inhibitors exert hemodynamic effects—natriuresis, reduction of intraglomerular pressure, and direct reduction of cardiac volume overload—that are particularly relevant to heart failure pathophysiology [4]. GLP-1 receptor agonists, by contrast, are posited to mitigate atherosclerotic risk through metabolic and anti-inflammatory pathways, including weight reduction, improved insulin sensitivity, and attenuation of vascular inflammation, mechanisms that have less direct bearing on heart failure hospitalization [4]. A narrative review of diabetic kidney disease similarly concluded that SGLT2 inhibitors appear more effective in mitigating glomerular hyperfiltration and lowering heart failure risk, whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists are particularly beneficial in reducing albuminuria and atherosclerotic cardiovascular events [14].

One caveat tempers the HHF finding. The global test for inconsistency in the network was significant for HHF (Q = 6.82, df = 2, p = 0.03), driven by the varying magnitudes of benefit observed in dedicated heart failure trials compared with general diabetes trials [4]. This means the indirect estimate for HHF may be less stable than the MACE estimate, and the true comparative effect could differ once direct head-to-head data become available.

## Contextualizing GLP-1 receptor agonist benefits on atherosclerotic events

The MACE equivalence should not be read as evidence that GLP-1 receptor agonists lack cardiovascular benefit. Both classes reduce MACE relative to placebo by 11–14%, and the mechanisms are complementary rather than redundant [4]. GLP-1 receptor agonists appear to act primarily through anti-atherogenic and anti-inflammatory pathways, which may translate into particular benefits for myocardial infarction and stroke—the atherosclerotic components of MACE—whereas SGLT2 inhibitors provide advantage through hemodynamic effects and enhanced cardiac energetics [4]. Real-world evidence supports similar effectiveness against myocardial infarction and stroke between the classes [4].

Observational data in the CKD population add a further dimension. A comparative effectiveness study of new users of SGLT2 inhibitors versus GLP-1 receptor agonists with mild-to-moderate CKD found that SGLT2 inhibitor use was associated with a significantly lower risk of the composite of death, myocardial infarction, or stroke (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.61–0.99), driven primarily by a reduction in non-fatal myocardial infarction (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.53–0.98), with no significant difference in non-fatal stroke (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.56–1.48) [1]. This observational signal, while subject to residual confounding, is consistent with the hypothesis that in patients with established kidney disease—a population at particularly high cardiovascular risk—SGLT2 inhibitors may offer incremental protection against atherosclerotic events beyond what the general-population trials suggest.

## Subgroup uncertainties

Several subgroup analyses qualify the headline findings. In patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (secondary prevention), both classes significantly reduced MACE with overlapping confidence intervals (SGLT2i HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.93; GLP-1RA HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.79–0.92), and the indirect comparison showed no difference (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.91–1.12; P = 0.86) [4]. In primary prevention patients with multiple risk factors but no established ASCVD, the benefit was attenuated for both classes (SGLT2i HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.83–1.07; GLP-1RA HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.77–1.08), and again no significant difference emerged between them (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.84–1.27; P = 0.77) [4].

Renal function modifies the comparative picture. SGLT2 inhibitors maintained their efficacy for heart failure hospitalization and renal protection across all eGFR subgroups, including patients with eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² [4]. In contrast, the cardiovascular benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists were attenuated in patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m²), although the number of events in this subgroup was small [4]. This is clinically relevant for the CKD population that is the focus of this report, as it suggests that the comparative advantage of SGLT2 inhibitors may widen as kidney function declines.

The composition of the underlying trials also introduces uncertainty. SGLT2 inhibitor trials actively recruited patients with heart failure (e.g., DAPA-HF, EMPEROR), whereas GLP-1 receptor agonist trials often excluded patients with severe heart failure (NYHA class III–IV), with a higher proportion of heart failure history in SGLT2i trial populations (22.4% versus 14.5%) [4]. This imbalance could inflate the apparent HHF advantage of SGLT2 inhibitors, though the magnitude of the effect (25% relative risk reduction) and its consistency across sensitivity analyses suggest it is unlikely to be entirely artifactual.

## Summary

The indirect evidence supports three conclusions. First, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists are broadly comparable for MACE prevention, with no statistically significant difference in the indirect comparison (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.94–1.13), a finding consistent across multiple network meta-analyses and sensitivity analyses. Second, SGLT2 inhibitors are significantly superior for preventing hospitalization for heart failure (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.66–0.85), a high-certainty finding with a plausible mechanistic basis. Third, GLP-1 receptor agonists retain meaningful atherosclerotic benefits, particularly in secondary prevention, and the comparative picture in CKD—where GLP-1 receptor agonists failed to separate from placebo for MACE in one analysis—warrants further investigation. The absence of head-to-head trials remains the central limitation, and the subgroup and inconsistency analyses underscore that these estimates, while the best available, carry residual uncertainty.

## Hypoglycemia Risk: No Significant Difference Between Classes

The comparative risk of hypoglycemia between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists is a question of considerable clinical importance, because both classes are increasingly used in patients with type 2 diabetes and concurrent chronic kidney disease (CKD), a population in whom hypoglycemia carries heightened cardiovascular and mortality risk. The most comprehensive evidence addressing this question comes from a systematic review and network meta-analysis of 14 large-scale randomized controlled trials encompassing 117,633 participants, which pooled data using a frequentist random-effects model [4]. That analysis found no statistically significant difference in the risk of severe hypoglycemia between the two drug classes, with a relative risk (RR) of 1.05 (95% CI 0.85–1.29) for SGLT2 inhibitors versus GLP-1 receptor agonists [4]. The confidence interval comfortably spans unity, indicating that the available network evidence does not support a meaningful differential in severe hypoglycemia risk between the classes.

The reassuring finding is not merely that the classes are comparable to one another, but that both are associated with low absolute risk relative to placebo. In the same network meta-analysis, the risk of severe hypoglycemia was low across all included trials and was not significantly increased by either drug class compared with placebo [4]. The pairwise estimates against placebo were remarkably similar: SGLT2 inhibitors carried an RR of 0.94 (95% CI 0.81–1.09), and GLP-1 receptor agonists an RR of 0.90 (95% CI 0.78–1.03), with both comparisons rated "neutral" in the analysis rather than favoring either active treatment or placebo [4]. The overlap of these estimates with the null value, and the near-identical point estimates, reinforce the conclusion that neither class meaningfully raises the risk of severe hypoglycemia in the populations studied, and that the indirect comparison between them is therefore essentially a comparison of two low-risk profiles.

This pattern is consistent with the broader safety literature for the individual classes. An overview of quantitative systematic reviews examining adverse events associated with SGLT2 inhibitors found that an increased risk of hypoglycemia was not consistently associated with dapagliflozin use relative to placebo or active comparators, with only 3 of 29 point estimates from seven reviews reaching statistical significance [7]. The same overview noted that a significantly increased risk of hypoglycemia was reported specifically in patients using a combination of dapagliflozin, metformin, and sulfonylureas [7] — a finding that points directly to the role of background therapy in determining hypoglycemia risk, a point developed below.

## The Role of Background Sulfonylurea Therapy

The low intrinsic hypoglycemia risk of both classes must be understood against the backdrop of the comparator and background therapies used in the underlying trials. The network meta-analysis reporting the RR of 1.05 was constructed around placebo-controlled trials, with placebo serving as the common comparator node in a star-shaped network geometry [4]. Within those trials, the assessment of severe hypoglycemia was made in the context of protocol-defined background glucose-lowering therapy, which in many large cardiovascular outcome trials included sulfonylureas and insulin. The near-null risk ratios against placebo therefore indicate that, even when added to background regimens that may themselves carry hypoglycemia risk, neither SGLT2 inhibitors nor GLP-1 receptor agonists materially increased the rate of severe hypoglycemic events.

Real-world evidence in the specific population of interest — patients with T2DM and CKD — directly supports the contrast between these classes and sulfonylureas. A retrospective cohort study of Medicare fee-for-service claims from 2012 to 2018 identified 18,567 adults with a mean age of 73 years who had both CKD and type 2 diabetes, of whom 14.0% (n = 2,528) had a prescription for an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 receptor agonist and 86.0% (n = 16,039) had a prescription for a sulfonylurea [9]. Using Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate the 90-day risk of hypoglycemia resulting in health care utilization, the study found that use of SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists was associated with a significantly lower risk of hypoglycemia compared with sulfonylureas, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.30 (95% CI 0.14–0.65) [9]. This more than threefold reduction in hypoglycemia requiring health care contact, observed specifically in a CKD population, underscores the extent to which the choice of background therapy — rather than the choice between the two newer classes — drives hypoglycemia risk in this vulnerable group.

The interaction between these newer agents and sulfonylurea background therapy is also documented in the meta-analytic literature. The overview of SGLT2 inhibitor safety reviews identified that the significantly increased risk of hypoglycemia observed in some analyses was confined to patients receiving the combination of dapagliflozin, metformin, and sulfonylureas [7]. This suggests that when hypoglycemia does occur in patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists, it is most plausibly attributable to the concurrent sulfonylurea (or insulin) rather than to the newer agent itself. The clinical implication is that, in patients with T2DM and CKD who are being switched to or initiated on either class, the highest-impact intervention for reducing hypoglycemia risk is the down-titration or discontinuation of sulfonylureas, and that the choice between an SGLT2 inhibitor and a GLP-1 receptor agonist is unlikely to materially alter that risk.

## The Absence of Head-to-Head Trials

A central limitation of the evidence base must be acknowledged: there are no head-to-head randomized controlled trials directly comparing SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists for hypoglycemia or any other outcome. The network meta-analysis reporting the RR of 1.05 (95% CI 0.85–1.29) explicitly noted that no head-to-head trials comparing the two classes were included in the primary analysis, and that the network geometry formed a star-shaped pattern with placebo as the sole common comparator [4]. The comparison between the classes is therefore entirely indirect, derived from the relative effects of each class against placebo. The GRADE certainty of evidence for the indirect comparison was rated as Moderate, downgraded specifically for indirectness due to the absence of head-to-head trials [4]. This is a structural feature of the entire comparative literature, not a limitation unique to the hypoglycemia analysis: other systematic reviews and network meta-analyses have reached the same conclusion, noting that all comparative findings are hypothesis-generating in the absence of direct randomized comparisons [12]. Observational studies have attempted to fill this gap, but they too are constrained by the lack of randomized head-to-head data and by the relatively low proportion of patients with CKD enrolled in most of the underlying cardiovascular outcome trials [1].

The implications of this indirectness for the hypoglycemia finding are worth specifying. Because the network estimate for the class comparison is built from two placebo-controlled estimates that are each close to the null, the precision of the indirect comparison is limited by the uncertainty in both constituent estimates. The width of the 95% CI for the class comparison (0.85–1.29) reflects this propagated uncertainty. A true difference between the classes at the extremes of that interval — for example, a 29% relative increase in severe hypoglycemia with SGLT2 inhibitors — cannot be excluded by the current evidence. However, the low absolute rates of severe hypoglycemia observed across all included trials [4] mean that even a relative difference at the upper bound of the confidence interval would translate into a small absolute difference in event rates. The clinical significance of the finding is therefore that neither class should be selected or avoided primarily on the basis of hypoglycemia risk.

## Context Within the CKD-Specific Evidence

The network meta-analysis that produced the headline RR of 1.05 included trials across the spectrum of type 2 diabetes, not exclusively CKD populations. For patients with established CKD specifically, the comparative evidence base is thinner but directionally consistent. A network meta-analysis restricted to patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD, comprising 13 studies and 32,949 patients, compared SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists on cardiovascular and renal outcomes [3]. While that analysis did not report a separate severe hypoglycemia comparison, it is the relevant efficacy backdrop for the CKD population, and its findings — that SGLT2 inhibitors reduced MACE and renal events while GLP-1 receptor agonists did not significantly reduce either in the primary analysis [3] — suggest that the efficacy considerations may differ between the classes in CKD more than the safety consideration of hypoglycemia does. The Medicare cohort study, which is the most directly relevant real-world evidence for hypoglycemia in CKD, found that both classes together were associated with markedly lower hypoglycemia risk than sulfonylureas [9], but it did not have sufficient statistical power to separate the two classes from one another (the 2,528 SGLT2i/GLP-1RA users were analyzed as a combined group).

## Interpretation

The available evidence supports a straightforward conclusion: in patients with type 2 diabetes and concurrent CKD, the risk of severe hypoglycemia does not differ significantly between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists, and both classes carry a low risk of severe hypoglycemia relative to placebo. The network meta-analysis estimate of RR 1.05 (95% CI 0.85–1.29) between the classes, with placebo-comparison estimates of 0.94 (0.81–1.09) for SGLT2 inhibitors and 0.90 (0.78–1.03) for GLP-1 receptor agonists, provides the quantitative basis for this conclusion [4]. The dominant driver of hypoglycemia risk in this population is background therapy, particularly sulfonylureas, as demonstrated by the Medicare CKD analysis (adjusted HR 0.30 for the newer classes versus sulfonylureas) [9] and by the meta-analytic observation that hypoglycemia risk emerges mainly when SGLT2 inhibitors are combined with sulfonylureas [7]. The principal caveat is the absence of head-to-head randomized trials, which confines the class comparison to indirect estimation and warrants the Moderate GRADE rating assigned to the indirect evidence [4]. For clinical decision-making, this means that hypoglycemia risk should not be the deciding factor between the two classes in patients with T2DM and CKD; instead, treatment selection should be guided by the differential efficacy profiles — including the greater benefit of SGLT2 inhibitors on heart failure hospitalization and renal outcomes — and by the differing non-hypoglycemic adverse event profiles, such as the higher risk of genital mycotic infections and diabetic ketoacidosis with SGLT2 inhibitors and the gastrointestinal effects associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists [4].

## Gastrointestinal Adverse Events: A GLP-1 Receptor Agonist Class Effect

Gastrointestinal (GI) adverse events constitute the most clinically distinctive tolerability liability of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), and the available evidence — spanning small head-to-head trials, large network meta-analyses, and real-world cohort studies — consistently identifies nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea as class-level effects that are essentially absent with SGLT2 inhibitors. This divergence carries particular weight in patients with type 2 diabetes and concurrent chronic kidney disease (CKD), where GI symptoms can precipitate dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, and pre-renal acute kidney injury in a population with already compromised renal reserve.

## Head-to-head evidence

The most direct comparative data come from a prospective head-to-head study of 60 patients per treatment group, which reported gastrointestinal symptoms — specifically nausea and vomiting — in 12% of GLP-1 agonist recipients versus 0% of SGLT2 inhibitor recipients [5]. The same trial reported no severe adverse events in either group, and the GI event difference was presented without a p-value, reflecting the small sample and the descriptive nature of the safety analysis [5]. While the 12% versus 0% contrast is striking, it must be interpreted with appropriate caution: the study was not powered for safety outcomes, the confidence interval around a zero-event rate is necessarily wide, and the single-center design limits generalizability. Nevertheless, the direction of the finding is consistent with the much larger body of evidence assembled through meta-analytic methods.

## Quantification from network meta-analyses

The most comprehensive quantification of the GI burden of GLP-1RAs comes from a Bayesian network meta-analysis of 48 randomized controlled trials involving 27,729 participants, which reported an overall incidence of GI adverse events of 11.66% across all GLP-1RA regimens [20]. Disaggregated by symptom, the incidence rates were: nausea 21.49%, diarrhea 10.62%, vomiting 9.10%, dyspepsia 8.67%, constipation 7.92%, and decreased appetite 5.49% [20]. Nausea was the most frequently reported GI adverse event, appearing in nearly all GLP-1RA treatment regimens, and the authors noted that the incidence figures reflect prevalence during treatment rather than cumulative exposure [20]. These rates substantially exceed the background GI symptom burden typically observed in placebo arms of diabetes trials, and they establish that GI intolerance is not an idiosyncratic reaction to a single agent but a pharmacological class effect.

A separate network meta-analysis of 14 large-scale cardiovascular outcome trials encompassing over 117,000 patients reached a congruent conclusion, noting that "GLP-1 receptor agonists were associated with gastrointestinal adverse events, though these were not the primary focus of our quantitative synthesis" [4]. The authors of that analysis explicitly framed the GI signal as one of the two defining safety distinctions between the classes, alongside the SGLT2 inhibitor-associated risks of genital mycotic infections and diabetic ketoacidosis [4]. The consistency of the GI finding across two methodologically distinct network meta-analyses — one focused on safety outcomes across 48 trials, the other on cardiorenal efficacy across 14 trials — strengthens the inference that the effect is robust.

## Within-class heterogeneity

The class effect label should not obscure meaningful differences among individual GLP-1RAs. The Bayesian network meta-analysis found that exenatide carried the highest incidence of nausea at 31.70%, significantly higher than other agents in the class, and also exhibited the highest vomiting incidence [20]. Tirzepatide — a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist — had the highest risk of inducing both nausea and diarrhea, while dulaglutide and lixisenatide had the lowest risks of these events [20]. Semaglutide demonstrated a significantly higher risk of diarrhea compared with other GLP-1RAs [20]. This heterogeneity has practical implications for drug selection in patients who are GI-sensitive, and it suggests that the class-level incidence figures may understate the burden for some agents while overstating it for others. It also complicates the interpretation of head-to-head comparisons that use a single agent from each class, since the choice of specific drugs can materially influence the magnitude of the GI difference observed.

## Real-world evidence

Real-world data extend the trial findings to routine clinical populations. An active-comparator, new-user cohort study of 141,080 veterans with type 2 diabetes who initiated a GLP-1RA (n = 19,765), an SGLT2 inhibitor (n = 75,058), or insulin glargine (n = 46,257) between 2018 and 2021 examined the risk of gastroparesis, intestinal obstruction, gallstones, acute cholecystitis, acute pancreatitis, and all-cause death using inverse probability weighted Cox regression [16]. The study design is notable for its size and for the breadth of GI outcomes assessed — extending beyond the nausea/vomiting/diarrhea triad to include more serious structural and inflammatory GI pathology. While the abstract excerpt available does not report the outcome-specific hazard ratios, the inclusion of these endpoints reflects growing recognition that GLP-1RA-related GI effects may extend beyond transient symptoms to clinically significant events requiring medical attention.

## Mechanistic basis and CKD-specific concerns

The mechanism underlying the GI adverse effect profile of GLP-1RAs is well established: activation of GLP-1 receptors in the gastrointestinal tract slows gastric emptying, while central GLP-1 receptor signaling in brainstem nuclei involved in emesis and satiety generates the nausea and vomiting that characterize the class. In patients with CKD, these effects acquire additional clinical significance. A network meta-analysis of randomized trials in type 2 diabetes with non-dialysis CKD noted that "gastrointestinal adverse reactions are the most common side effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists, which can cause significant fluid loss and pre-renal acute failure" [10]. The same analysis documented that between April 2005 and August 2008, 78 cases of renal disease caused by exenatide were reported to the FDA, illustrating the potential for GI-mediated volume depletion to translate into renal injury [10]. A separate review of diabetic kidney disease similarly cautioned that GLP-1RAs "are commonly associated with gastrointestinal adverse events, which may lead to dehydration in some people" [14]. However, the same review noted a post hoc analysis of semaglutide trials that found no increased risk of kidney-related adverse events compared with active comparators, suggesting that the dehydration risk is manageable with appropriate monitoring and dose titration in most patients [14]. The FLOW trial further demonstrated that semaglutide reduced cardiovascular risk regardless of baseline CKD severity, indicating that the GI tolerability burden does not negate the renal and cardiovascular benefits of the class in this population [14].

## Absence of a GI signal with SGLT2 inhibitors

In contrast to the well-characterized GI profile of GLP-1RAs, SGLT2 inhibitors show no consistent GI signal. In the head-to-head trial, the SGLT2 inhibitor group reported 0% gastrointestinal adverse events, with the class's characteristic adverse event being urinary tract infection at 8% [5]. The 14-trial network meta-analysis confirmed that the safety liabilities of SGLT2 inhibitors lie elsewhere: a more than three-fold increase in the risk of genital mycotic infections compared with placebo (RR = 3.49, 95% CI = 2.63–4.55) and a two-fold rise in the risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (RR = 2.24, 95% CI = 1.45–3.46) [4]. An observational study comparing SGLT2 inhibitors with GLP-1RAs in patients with cardiovascular disease similarly reported twofold increases in lower-limb amputation and diabetic ketoacidosis with SGLT2 inhibitor use, but no excess of other serious adverse events of special concern [13]. The GI tolerability advantage of SGLT2 inhibitors is therefore not a neutral absence of symptoms but a clinically meaningful difference that shapes the tolerability calculus, particularly in patients who have experienced GI intolerance to other medications or who have baseline gastrointestinal comorbidity.

## Clinical implications

For clinicians selecting between these classes in patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD, the GI adverse event profile of GLP-1RAs argues for several practical measures: slow dose titration to mitigate nausea, patient education about the transient nature of symptoms in many cases, and heightened vigilance for volume depletion in patients with reduced eGFR, particularly during intercurrent illness or in hot climates. The within-class heterogeneity provides an additional lever — choosing an agent with a lower nausea incidence (e.g., dulaglutide or lixisenatide) may improve tolerability in GI-sensitive patients [20]. Conversely, the absence of a GI signal with SGLT2 inhibitors does not mean the class is free of adverse events; the genital mycotic infection and DKA risks require their own monitoring strategies [4][13]. The two classes thus present complementary rather than overlapping safety profiles, and the GI burden of GLP-1RAs — while substantial and class-defining — must be weighed against the SGLT2 inhibitor risks of genital infections, DKA, and amputation in the context of each individual patient's risk factors and preferences.

## Genital Mycotic Infections and Urinary Tract Infections: SGLT2 Inhibitor Risks

### The Genital Mycotic Infection Signal: Magnitude and Consistency

The most robust and consistent safety difference between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in type 2 diabetes concerns genital mycotic infections. A 2026 systematic review and network meta-analysis of 14 large-scale RCTs (over 117,000 participants) quantified the class-level risk: SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with a relative risk of 3.56 (95% CI 2.80–4.53) for genital mycotic infections compared with placebo, while GLP-1 receptor agonists showed no increase (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.85–1.22) [4]. The indirect head-to-head comparison between the two classes yielded an RR of 3.49 (95% CI 2.63–4.55), meaning a patient treated with an SGLT2 inhibitor faces roughly three-and-a-half times the risk of a genital mycotic infection compared with a patient treated with a GLP-1 receptor agonist [4]. The same analysis rated the certainty of this safety signal as High, with an absolute effect estimate of 249 more events per 1,000 patients (95% CI 163 to 355 more) [4]. This GRADE rating is notable because it was not downgraded for indirectness, in contrast to several efficacy outcomes in the same analysis that were downgraded precisely because no head-to-head trials exist [4].

The magnitude of this signal is not an artefact of a single meta-analysis. An overview of quantitative systematic reviews, published in 2021, examined 47 reviews of SGLT2 inhibitor safety and found that genital tract infections were consistently associated with canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, and empagliflozin versus placebo, with point estimates ranging from 2.5 to 9.8, and versus other antihyperglycemic agents with point estimates ranging from 2.7 to 12.0 [7]. The same overview reported that 32 of 48 point estimates (from 12 reviews) for dapagliflozin, 34 of 37 point estimates (from 12 reviews) for canagliflozin, and 24 of 38 point estimates (from 9 reviews) for empagliflozin were statistically significant for genital tract infections [7]. A living systematic review and network meta-analysis of medications for type 2 diabetes similarly reported an odds ratio of 3.29 (95% CI 2.88–3.77) for genital infections with SGLT2 inhibitors, rated as high certainty [29]. The convergence of these independent syntheses — a 2026 NMA, a 2021 umbrella review, and a living systematic review — makes the genital mycotic infection signal one of the best-established adverse effects in this drug class.

### Mechanism and Biological Plausibility

The mechanism is well understood and consistent with the class effect. SGLT2 inhibitors reduce renal glucose reabsorption, producing glycosuria — the excretion of glucose into the urine. This increased glucose exposure through the genitourinary tract is postulated to be the basis for the increase in genital infections as a class effect [7]. The glucose-rich environment promotes fungal overgrowth, particularly of Candida species, in the genital region. This mechanism explains why the risk is highest in women and in uncircumcised men, where moisture and glucose exposure are greater [7]. It also explains a clinically important nuance in the CKD population: because glycosuria is reduced as kidney function declines, the risk of genital mycotic infections is lower in patients with chronic kidney disease than in those with preserved eGFR, though it remains elevated relative to placebo [7]. This attenuation with declining eGFR is consistent with the mechanism — less filtered glucose means less glucose reaching the urine — and is a point worth emphasising when counselling patients with CKD, who may otherwise be deterred by the infection risk.

### Urinary Tract Infections: A More Contested Signal

In contrast to the consistent genital mycotic infection signal, the evidence for urinary tract infections (UTIs) with SGLT2 inhibitors is mixed and agent-specific. The 2021 overview of systematic reviews found that dapagliflozin was the only SGLT2 inhibitor that exhibited a statistically significant increased risk of UTIs [7]. The same overview reported that 22 of 79 point estimates (from 14 reviews) for dapagliflozin were significantly increased for UTIs versus placebo and active comparators, whereas empagliflozin showed a significant increase in UTIs versus dapagliflozin in only 4% of comparisons (3 of 71 point estimates from 12 reviews) [7]. This agent-level heterogeneity is important: it suggests that UTI risk is not a uniform class effect but may be specific to certain molecules, possibly related to differences in selectivity, dosing, or the degree of glycosuria produced.

A narrative review of SGLT2 inhibitors in type 2 diabetes with CKD contextualised the UTI question with data from large trials and meta-analyses. In the EMPA-REG OUTCOME trial, over 30% of women in the placebo group experienced a UTI, but this was not increased by the SGLT2 inhibitor [2]. A meta-analysis by Staplin and colleagues found only a non-significant 7% increase in the risk of UTIs in patients taking SGLT2 inhibitors [2]. A population-based cohort study of more than 200,000 patients found no difference in the risk of UTI events between patients initiating SGLT2 inhibitors compared with those initiating DPP-4 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists [2]. These data suggest that the baseline risk of UTIs in the diabetic population is high — particularly in women — and that SGLT2 inhibitors add little or nothing to that baseline risk at the class level.

### Conflicting Network Meta-Analysis Results

The picture becomes more complicated when network meta-analyses are considered. A 2025 network meta-analysis of SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and finerenone in type 2 diabetes with non-dialysis CKD reported a higher risk of urinary tract infections in patients treated with GLP-1 receptor agonists compared with those receiving SGLT2 inhibitors — a finding the authors themselves acknowledged "contradicts previous research findings" [10]. This result is difficult to reconcile with the class-level evidence from the overview of systematic reviews and the population-based cohort, which found no difference or a slight increase with SGLT2 inhibitors [2][7]. The discrepancy likely reflects differences in how UTIs were defined (all UTIs versus serious UTIs), the specific agents compared, the patient populations (CKD-specific versus general type 2 diabetes), and the indirect nature of the comparison [10].

A small comparative study reported UTIs in 8% of SGLT2 inhibitor users versus 0% of GLP-1 agonist users, but this was a short-term study with limited sample size and does not carry the weight of the larger syntheses [5]. An observational study in patients with type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease found no significant increased risk of serious urinary tract infection associated with SGLT2 inhibitors versus GLP-1 receptor agonists (HR not reported in the available excerpt, but the study concluded no significant increase) [13]. The distinction between "all UTIs" and "serious UTIs" is critical: the observational data suggest that even if SGLT2 inhibitors modestly increase the frequency of UTIs, they do not increase the risk of serious infections requiring hospitalisation [13].

### Explaining the Discrepancies

Several factors explain the conflicting results across studies of UTI risk. First, definitions vary: some studies capture all UTIs, including asymptomatic bacteriuria and mild cystitis, while others capture only serious UTIs requiring hospitalisation. The observational study that found no increase specifically examined serious UTIs [13], whereas the small comparative study that found 8% versus 0% likely captured all UTIs [5]. Second, the agents differ: dapagliflozin appears to carry a higher UTI signal than empagliflozin or canagliflozin [7], so pooling agents into a class-level estimate can obscure or dilute agent-specific effects. Third, the baseline risk differs by sex: women with diabetes have a high baseline risk of UTIs, as illustrated by the EMPA-REG OUTCOME data showing over 30% of women in the placebo group experienced a UTI [2]. When the baseline rate is this high, the incremental risk attributable to the drug is difficult to detect and may be clinically less important. Fourth, the CKD population may differ: reduced glycosuria in CKD could attenuate any UTI risk, just as it attenuates genital mycotic infection risk [7]. Finally, the 2025 NMA finding of higher UTI risk with GLP-1 receptor agonists is an outlier that the authors themselves flagged as contradicting prior research, and it may reflect the specific trial populations or definitions used in the included studies [10].

### Clinical Implications

For clinical practice, the safety profiles of the two classes diverge in a predictable way. SGLT2 inhibitors carry a well-established, high-certainty risk of genital mycotic infections — roughly 3.5-fold higher than GLP-1 receptor agonists and placebo — with the risk highest in women and uncircumcised men, and attenuated but not eliminated in CKD [4][7]. GLP-1 receptor agonists show no increased risk of genital mycotic infections versus placebo (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.85–1.22) [4]. For UTIs, the evidence does not support a strong class-level effect: the pooled increase is non-significant at 7% [2], dapagliflozin is the only agent with a consistent signal [7], and serious UTIs are not increased in observational data [13]. The conflicting network meta-analysis result — suggesting higher UTI risk with GLP-1 receptor agonists — should be interpreted cautiously given its acknowledged contradiction of prior evidence [10].

The practical implication is that genital mycotic infections are a predictable, manageable, and dose-limiting adverse effect of SGLT2 inhibitors, whereas UTI risk is a weaker and more contested signal that should not, on current evidence, be a primary determinant of drug choice. In patients with CKD, the reduced glycosuria associated with impaired kidney function lowers — but does not eliminate — the genital infection risk, which may make SGLT2 inhibitors more tolerable in this population than in patients with preserved eGFR [7]. Clinicians should counsel patients about the likelihood of genital mycotic infections, particularly women, and consider prophylactic or early treatment strategies, while recognising that the cardiovascular and renal benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors in CKD are substantial and generally outweigh the infection risk [2][4].

## Efficacy and Safety by Baseline eGFR: Subgroup Insights

### Subgroup Analyses from the Network Meta-Analysis

The most direct evidence on eGFR-stratified efficacy comes from a 2026 systematic review and network meta-analysis of 14 large-scale placebo-controlled cardiovascular outcome trials (117,633 participants) that explicitly stratified results by baseline kidney function [4]. In the subgroup analysis, SGLT2 inhibitors showed a significantly greater relative risk reduction in MACE among patients with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² compared with those with eGFR ≥60, with an interaction p-value of 0.04 [4]. For the composite renal outcome, the interaction was stronger still, at p<0.01 [4]. In the eGFR ≥60 subgroup, the SGLT2i-versus-placebo hazard ratio for MACE was 0.82 (95% CI 0.70–0.95), while the corresponding GLP-1 receptor agonist hazard ratio was 0.84 (95% CI 0.72–0.97) [4].

The direction of these interactions matters for interpretation. The source notes that the MACE interaction (p=0.04) indicates a greater relative risk reduction in lower eGFR [4]. For renal efficacy (p<0.01), the authors state that SGLT2 inhibitors show consistent renal protection across eGFR levels, but that the absolute benefit is higher in the ≥60 subgroup due to higher event rates [4]. This last claim sits awkwardly against a second passage in the same analysis reporting that "the absolute benefit was often greater in those with lower eGFR due to higher event rates" [4]. The two statements are contradictory, and the discrepancy likely reflects a rendering artifact in the table footnote (the "60" may denote either the ≥60 or the <60 column) rather than a substantive analytical difference. The relative-risk interaction itself — the p=0.04 for MACE and p<0.01 for renal composite — is reported consistently and is the figure the chapter relies on.

A complementary network meta-regression in the same analysis identified baseline eGFR as a significant effect modifier for the composite renal outcome (p=0.04), with greater relative risk reductions observed in trials enrolling patients with preserved kidney function, although the absolute benefit was often greater in those with lower eGFR due to higher event rates [4]. This meta-regression p-value (0.04) differs from the subgroup interaction p-value (<0.01) for the same outcome, reflecting the different statistical approaches — one comparing categorical eGFR subgroups, the other treating eGFR as a continuous trial-level covariate. Both point in the same direction: SGLT2 inhibitors work across the eGFR spectrum, with the balance of relative and absolute benefit shifting by baseline kidney function.

### The eGFR <30 Frontier

The same network meta-analysis reported that SGLT2 inhibitors maintained their efficacy for renal protection and heart failure hospitalization across all eGFR subgroups, including patients with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² [4]. The contrast with GLP-1 receptor agonists is sharp: the cardiovascular benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists were attenuated in patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m²), although the number of events in this subgroup was small [4]. The authors note that recent evidence suggests GLP-1 receptor agonists may offer specific advantages in patients with preserved eGFR, whereas SGLT2 inhibitors demonstrate greater efficacy in those with established renal impairment [4].

The landmark kidney-outcome trials support the maintained efficacy of SGLT2 inhibitors at low eGFR. In EMPA-KIDNEY, which enrolled patients with CKD with or without diabetes, empagliflozin consistently slowed eGFR decline compared with placebo across all subgroups [14]. The trial's prespecified subgroup analysis showed smaller relative risk reductions among participants with lower baseline albuminuria, likely due to a lower event rate [14]. The EMPEROR-Reduced trial, which included patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (half with diabetes, half with established CKD), reported a renal composite outcome hazard ratio of 0.50 (95% CI 0.32–0.77, P < 0.01) with empagliflozin, an effect independent of diabetes status and baseline kidney function [2].

The regulatory and guideline landscape has shifted accordingly. When SGLT2 inhibitors were first developed, they were contraindicated in patients with impaired kidney function because of attenuated glucosuria and limited glycemic effects [2]. That position has been reversed by trial data demonstrating clear kidney and cardiac benefits in CKD regardless of diabetes status [2]. Most guidelines now recommend SGLT2 initiation down to an eGFR of 30 mL/min/1.73 m², and the US Food and Drug Administration advises that dapagliflozin can be initiated down to an eGFR of >25 mL/min/1.73 m² [2]. The eGFR <30 subgroup is therefore no longer a contraindication for SGLT2 inhibitors, and the network meta-analysis finding of maintained efficacy in this range aligns with the regulatory evolution.

### GLP-1 Receptor Agonists in Renal Impairment

The attenuation of GLP-1 receptor agonist cardiovascular benefits at eGFR <30 does not mean the class lacks kidney effects. Post hoc analyses from the LEADER (liraglutide) and SUSTAIN-6 (semaglutide) cardiovascular outcome trials demonstrated that both agents significantly reduced albuminuria and slowed eGFR decline in people with type 2 diabetes, especially those with baseline eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² or preexisting albuminuria [14]. The mechanism appears to differ from SGLT2 inhibitors: GLP-1 receptor agonists primarily reduce the onset and progression of albuminuria, whereas SGLT2 inhibitors appear more effective in mitigating glomerular hyperfiltration and lowering heart failure risk [14]. A network meta-analysis focused on CKD flagged exenatide as a drug that "should be minimized in patients with severe renal insufficiency" [10], underscoring that within-class differences exist for GLP-1 receptor agonists in advanced CKD.

The eGFR-stratified pattern in the network meta-analysis — SGLT2 inhibitors maintaining efficacy at low eGFR, GLP-1 receptor agonists losing cardiovascular benefit at eGFR <30 — is consistent with the class-level renal outcome data. In the CKD-focused network meta-analysis of 13 studies (32,949 patients), SGLT2 inhibitors reduced MACE (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.75–0.96) and renal events (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.59–0.78), while GLP-1 receptor agonists did not reach significance for either outcome in that population (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.80–1.04 for MACE; RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.72–1.03 for renal events) [3]. The subgroup analysis adds granularity: the SGLT2 inhibitor advantage is most pronounced at lower eGFR, while GLP-1 receptor agonists retain their cardiovascular benefits primarily at preserved eGFR [4].

### Absolute Versus Relative Risk: Interpreting the Interaction

A significant interaction p-value means the relative risk reduction differs by eGFR subgroup, but it does not by itself tell the clinician which patient derives more absolute benefit. The network meta-analysis authors are explicit that for the renal composite, SGLT2 inhibitors show consistent renal protection across eGFR levels [4]. The observational literature reinforces the relative-versus-absolute distinction. A real-world study of patients with T2DM and CKD stages G1–G3 found that when stratified by CKD stage, patients with albuminuria and normal or near-normal eGFR appeared to experience the greatest relative benefit of SGLT2 inhibitors, although overall event rates in patients with CKD stages G1/G2 were lower than among patients with more advanced CKD [1]. In other words, the largest relative risk reduction may occur where the baseline risk is lowest — a pattern that argues for treating lower-eGFR patients despite smaller relative effects, because their higher absolute risk converts a smaller relative reduction into a larger number of events prevented.

### Safety Outcomes by eGFR: An Evidence Gap

Safety outcomes stratified by baseline eGFR are not reported in the available evidence. The network meta-analysis reports overall safety comparisons — SGLT2 inhibitors carry an elevated risk of genital infections (RR 3.49, 95% CI 2.63–4.55) and diabetic ketoacidosis (RR 2.36, 95% CI 1.33–4.17) compared with GLP-1 receptor agonists [4] — but these are not broken down by eGFR category. The narrative review of SGLT2 inhibitors in CKD discusses safety in patients with CKD generally, noting that SGLT2 inhibitors are generally well tolerated in patients with T2D and CKD [2], but again without eGFR-stratified safety data. No source provides hypoglycemia, gastrointestinal, or acute kidney injury outcomes stratified by eGFR for the SGLT2i-versus-GLP-1RA comparison.

This is a genuine gap. The efficacy evidence supports use of SGLT2 inhibitors across the eGFR spectrum down to <30, and the safety profile in CKD is reassuring at the class level, but whether the relative risks of genital infections or DKA vary by eGFR category cannot be determined from the available evidence. Given that glycosuria — the mechanism driving genital infections — is attenuated at lower eGFR, one might expect lower infection risk in advanced CKD, but no source provides the stratified data to confirm this. The chapter therefore cannot state a safety-by-eGFR profile for either class; it can only note that the evidence base is silent on this question.

### Implications for Clinical Decision-Making

The eGFR-stratified findings reinforce the class-level guidance. SGLT2 inhibitors are preferred in people with heart failure or at high risk for heart failure, whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists are favored for those requiring significant weight loss, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk reduction, or mitigation of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [14]. In the specific context of CKD, the evidence favors SGLT2 inhibitors for kidney outcomes [3][4], and the subgroup data add precision: the SGLT2 inhibitor advantage is most pronounced at lower eGFR, while GLP-1 receptor agonists retain their cardiovascular benefits primarily at preserved eGFR [4].

The combination of the two classes is mechanistically supported — co-administration leads to greater reductions in albuminuria and more pronounced initial declines in eGFR, indicative of reduced intraglomerular pressure [14] — and the eGFR-stratified data suggest complementary roles rather than competition: SGLT2 inhibitors for renal and heart-failure protection across the eGFR spectrum, GLP-1 receptor agonists for atherosclerotic and albuminuria benefits where eGFR is preserved [4][14]. For the clinician managing a patient with T2DM and CKD, the interaction p-values provide a quantitative basis for the intuitive practice of prioritizing SGLT2 inhibitors as kidney function declines, while reserving GLP-1 receptor agonists as the primary cardiovascular agent in patients with preserved eGFR or as an add-on for weight and atherosclerotic risk.

## Limitations of the Evidence Base: Indirectness, Heterogeneity, and Transitivity

### The Absence of Head-to-Head Evidence

The most fundamental limitation of the comparative evidence base for SGLT2 inhibitors versus GLP-1 receptor agonists in type 2 diabetes with chronic kidney disease is the complete absence of direct randomized comparisons. No head-to-head randomized controlled trial comparing the two classes in this population has been identified, a finding confirmed by multiple independent syntheses. A 2026 systematic review and network meta-analysis of large-scale, placebo-controlled cardiovascular outcome trials, which searched PubMed, Embase, and CENTRAL up to December 2025, explicitly states that no head-to-head trials comparing SGLT2 inhibitors with GLP-1 receptor agonists were included in its primary analysis [4]. A second network meta-analysis focused on patients with a history of myocardial infarction similarly reports that "no head-to-head randomized trials directly comparing SGLT2i and GLP-1 RAs exist," with all comparative statements derived from separate placebo-controlled trials [12]. A 2026 narrative review of diabetic kidney disease reaches the same conclusion, noting that "direct head-to-head trials are lacking" despite indirect comparisons suggesting SGLT2 inhibitors may offer greater protection against kidney function decline [14].

This absence is not a minor gap but a structural feature of the entire evidence base. Every comparative estimate in the literature — whether for major adverse cardiovascular events, heart failure hospitalization, renal composite outcomes, or safety endpoints — is derived from indirect comparisons. The clinical consequence is that clinicians choosing between the two classes for a patient with type 2 diabetes and CKD must rely on inference across separate placebo-controlled trials rather than on direct evidence of relative efficacy. As one source puts it, all comparative statements between classes "should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive" [12].

### Indirect Comparisons and Star-Shaped Networks

The reliance on indirect comparisons shapes the statistical architecture of the evidence. The 2026 network meta-analysis included 14 randomized controlled trials comparing SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists with placebo, forming a star-shaped network with placebo as the common comparator node for all active treatments [4]. This geometry has a critical statistical consequence: because there are no closed loops in the network, formal testing of inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence is impossible [12]. In a network with head-to-head arms, one can statistically check whether the indirect estimate agrees with the direct estimate; in a star-shaped network, no such check is possible. The 2026 analysis did perform a global inconsistency test for the primary outcome of MACE (Q = 3.54, df = 2, p = 0.17), which did not reveal significant inconsistency, but for the secondary outcome of hospitalization for heart failure the global test indicated potential inconsistency (Q = 6.82, df = 2, p = 0.03), driven by the varying magnitudes of benefit observed in dedicated heart failure trials compared to general diabetes trials [4]. Local inconsistency was assessed using node-splitting, but for the comparison of SGLT2 inhibitors versus GLP-1 receptor agonists no direct evidence was available, so the consistency check relied entirely on agreement between indirect estimates derived from placebo-controlled trials [4].

The transitivity assumption — the requirement that the populations in the placebo-controlled trials be sufficiently similar to justify indirect comparison — is therefore the load-bearing assumption of the entire comparative evidence base. The 2026 analysis states that the transitivity assumption, "while statistically supported, is challenged by slight variation in baseline characteristics" [4]. This is a carefully worded qualification; the variation is not slight in all domains, as discussed below.

### Population Heterogeneity: Heart Failure and Kidney Function

The most consequential differences between the trial populations feeding into the indirect comparisons concern heart failure prevalence and kidney function. The 2026 network meta-analysis reports that SGLT2 inhibitor trials included a weighted mean history of heart failure of 22.4% of participants, compared with 14.5% in GLP-1 receptor agonist trials — a standardized difference exceeding 0.2, which the authors classify as "dissimilar" [4]. The explanation is structural: SGLT2 inhibitor trials actively recruited heart failure patients (DAPA-HF, EMPEROR-Reduced), whereas GLP-1 receptor agonist trials often excluded severe heart failure (NYHA class III–IV) [4]. This difference matters because heart failure status is a powerful effect modifier for both classes, and it directly threatens the transitivity assumption for any comparison involving heart failure outcomes.

Kidney function differences are equally pronounced. The SGLT2 inhibitor node included dedicated renal outcome trials (CREDENCE, DAPA-CKD, EMPA-KIDNEY) and heart failure trials, resulting in a lower weighted mean eGFR of 65.2 mL/min/1.73 m², compared with 76.8 mL/min/1.73 m² for the GLP-1 receptor agonist node [4]. This 11.6 mL/min/1.73 m² difference in baseline kidney function is clinically substantial and directly relevant to a report focused on patients with concurrent CKD. The GLP-1 receptor agonist trials also had a slightly higher prevalence of established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (76.5% versus 68.3%) [4]. These differences were addressed through prespecified subgroup analyses and meta-regression, but the authors acknowledge that "residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out" [4].

The implications for the CKD population are significant. Because the SGLT2 inhibitor trials enrolled patients with more advanced kidney disease, the class-level estimates for SGLT2 inhibitors are more directly applicable to patients with CKD than are the GLP-1 receptor agonist estimates, which derive from populations with relatively preserved kidney function. A separate CKD-focused network meta-analysis, which searched through November 2022 and included trials with at least 24 weeks of follow-up, found that SGLT2 inhibitors reduced MACE and renal events in the CKD population whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists did not reach significance in that population [11]. This finding may reflect genuine differences in class efficacy in CKD, or it may reflect the different baseline characteristics of the trials feeding each node — a distinction that cannot be resolved without head-to-head data.

### Trial Duration and Follow-Up Variability

Trial durations and follow-up periods vary substantially across the trials contributing to the indirect comparisons. The 2026 network meta-analysis restricted inclusion to large-scale, placebo-controlled cardiovascular outcome trials, which by design have longer follow-up than typical registration trials, but the durations still differ meaningfully across trials [4]. The myocardial infarction-focused network meta-analysis notes that follow-up ranged from relatively short periods in some trials to 5.4 years in the DECLARE-TIMI 58 myocardial infarction subgroup, and that this variability "does not perfectly align" with the focus of the review [12]. Some analyses explicitly excluded trials with follow-up under 1.5 years to reduce heterogeneity [4]. The practical consequence is that comparative estimates pool effects measured over different time horizons, which matters for outcomes with different time courses — renal progression develops over years, whereas heart failure hospitalization can occur earlier. A drug that shows benefit at 1 year may or may not sustain that benefit at 5 years, and pooling across different durations can obscure such temporal patterns.

### Certainty of Evidence: GRADE Ratings

The GRADE certainty of evidence for comparative efficacy outcomes is outcome-specific rather than uniform, and the ratings reflect the indirectness inherent in the evidence base. The 2026 network meta-analysis provides explicit GRADE ratings for the two principal efficacy outcomes. For major adverse cardiovascular events, the comparative estimate for SGLT2 inhibitors versus GLP-1 receptor agonists (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.94–1.13) was rated Moderate, downgraded once for indirectness because of the absence of head-to-head trials [4]. For hospitalization for heart failure, the comparative estimate (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.66–0.85) was rated High, reflecting the large magnitude of the effect and consistency across sensitivity analyses [4]. This asymmetry is notable: the evidence supporting a comparative benefit for SGLT2 inhibitors in heart failure hospitalization is rated higher than the evidence for the null MACE comparison, even though both derive from the same indirect network. The authors justify the High rating for heart failure hospitalization on the basis of effect size and consistency, but the indirectness downgrade applied to MACE would logically apply to all outcomes in the network. The detailed GRADE summary for all outcomes is provided in Table 11 of the source, which was not included in the available excerpts [4].

The certainty of evidence for safety outcomes is less explicitly characterized. The same source reports that GLP-1 receptor agonists have a lower risk of genital infections and ketoacidosis, but does not state a GRADE certainty level for these comparisons in the available text [4]. The myocardial infarction-focused network meta-analysis applied a double indirectness downgrade: evidence was downgraded by one level for indirectness in all direct comparisons (because even the placebo-controlled trials enrolled populations that did not perfectly match the review's focus), and "an additional level of indirectness was applied owing to the absence of head-to-head trials" for the indirect NMA comparisons between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists [12]. This double downgrade reflects a general principle: indirect comparisons inherit all the indirectness of their component trials plus an additional layer from the absence of direct evidence.

### Implications for Interpretation and the Case for Direct Trials

These limitations have concrete interpretive consequences. First, the comparative estimates for renal outcomes — where SGLT2 inhibitors appear superior (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.66–0.87 for composite renal outcomes versus GLP-1 receptor agonists) — must be read as indirect estimates that could change with direct evidence [4]. The same applies to the null finding for MACE (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.94–1.13) and the heart failure hospitalization benefit (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.66–0.85) [4]. Second, the population differences described above mean that even a well-conducted indirect comparison may not generalize to specific patient subgroups. The myocardial infarction-focused analysis, for example, found that evidence for cardiovascular death was limited, with only two SGLT2 inhibitor trials contributing data, and concluded that treatment selection should be individualized [12]. Third, the star-shaped network geometry precludes formal inconsistency testing, meaning that undetected violations of the transitivity assumption could bias comparative estimates in unknown directions [12].

The need for direct trials is therefore not a methodological nicety but a prerequisite for confident clinical decision-making. The 2026 review notes that "large-scale randomized trials evaluating this combination are necessary" for combination therapy, and the same logic applies with greater force to head-to-head monotherapy comparisons [4]. A direct trial would need to enroll patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD, randomize them to an SGLT2 inhibitor or a GLP-1 receptor agonist on top of standard of care, and follow them for renal and cardiovascular outcomes over several years. Such a trial would resolve the transitivity question, allow formal consistency testing, and provide direct evidence on comparative safety outcomes — including the genital infection and ketoacidosis signals that currently rest on indirect comparisons [4]. Until such trials are conducted, the comparative evidence base for these two classes in type 2 diabetes with CKD will remain moderate-to-low certainty for most efficacy outcomes, with the notable exception of the heart failure hospitalization benefit, and all comparative statements should be interpreted with the caveats described above [4][12].

## Alignment with Clinical Guidelines: ADA and KDIGO Recommendations

### The Shared Foundation: Both Classes, Both Guidelines

The convergence of the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) on the same two drug classes for type 2 diabetes with chronic kidney disease is the single most important guideline development of the past decade for this population. Both bodies now recommend SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists as preferred pharmacotherapy, a position that reflects the accumulation of cardiovascular- and kidney-outcome trial evidence rather than glycemic potency alone. [4] The 2025 network meta-analysis in *Cureus* (PMC12781563) frames the recommendation directly: "International guidelines recommend these medications as first-line therapies for patients with T2DM and confirmed atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), heart failure, or CKD, recognizing the established efficacy of SGLT2 inhibitors across the full spectrum of heart failure phenotypes." [4]

The 2025 review in *Diabetes & Metabolism Journal* confirms the dual recommendation: "Current guidelines recommend either class for people with type 2 diabetes mellitus and established ASCVD, high cardiovascular risk, HF, or CKD." [14] A 2025 network meta-analysis in *Frontiers in Pharmacology* adds that "SGLT-2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and Finerenone are recommended as preferred pharmacotherapies for T2DM patients with CKD," noting that the guidelines do not specify a clear hierarchy of preference among the options. [10]

This shared recommendation is not a trivial alignment. The two classes operate through different mechanisms — SGLT2 inhibitors restore tubuloglomerular feedback and reduce intraglomerular pressure, while GLP-1 receptor agonists act through weight reduction, improved insulin sensitivity, and attenuation of vascular inflammation [4] — yet the guideline bodies have converged on the same two classes for the same high-risk population.

### ADINGO's Explicit Hierarchy: SGLT2 Inhibitors as First-Line

Where the two bodies diverge is the hierarchy they assign. KDIGO has been unmistakable: SGLT2 inhibitors are first-line therapy alongside metformin. A narrative review of SGLT2 inhibitors in CKD states: "recent KDIGO guidelines now consider SGLT2 inhibitors as first-line therapy, alongside metformin, for the management of patients with T2D and CKD." [2] The same review elaborates that KDIGO has recommended SGLT2 inhibitors as first-line therapy in patients with CKD "alongside statins, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors and multifactorial risk factor management as indicated." [2] The positioning of SGLT2 inhibitors alongside RAS inhibitors is the critical structural point: KDIGO treats them as foundational kidney-protective therapy, not merely as glucose-lowering agents to be added later.

The 2023 KDIGO CKD guideline extended this recommendation beyond diabetes. A bibliometric analysis in *Frontiers in Medicine* (2026) states: "the 2023 KDIGO CKD guidelines recommend SGLT2 inhibitors as first-line kidney-protective agents in patients with CKD stages G2–G4 (eGFR ≥20 mL/min/1.73 m²), irrespective of diabetes status or baseline UACR levels, thereby establishing their preeminence over other non-RAS inhibitor therapies." [18] The eGFR threshold has moved progressively downward: the 2020 KDIGO diabetes-and-CKD guideline recommended SGLT2 inhibitors for eGFR >30 mL/min/1.73 m² "at any level of current glycemic control" [24], and the 2023 guideline lowered the floor to ≥20. [18] The narrative review notes that "most guidelines recommend SGLT2 initiation down to an eGFR of 30 mL/min/1.73 m²," with the US FDA now advising dapagliflozin can be initiated down to eGFR >25, and predicts that "future changes to KDIGO guidelines are likely to incorporate recommendations for lower eGFR limits for SGLT2 inhibitor use in CKD." [2]

The rationale for KDIGO's first-line placement is the kidney-outcome trial evidence: DAPA-CKD, CREDENCE, and EMPA-KIDNEY were all stopped early because of unequivocal evidence of benefit. [2] The network meta-analysis supports this renal primacy, finding SGLT2 inhibitors significantly more effective than GLP-1 receptor agonists for composite renal outcomes (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.66–0.87, SGLT2i vs GLP-1RA) [4], and a CKD-focused network meta-analysis from 2021 found that "SGLT-2 inhibitors were associated with a decreased risk of cardiovascular and renal events, but GLP-1 RAs were not" in patients with CKD. [3]

### ADA's Non-Hierarchical Approach

The ADA, by contrast, gives no strict hierarchy. The *Frontiers in Pharmacology* network meta-analysis observes: "the ADA recommends that individuals with T2DM and CKD should use SGLT-2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists as their first choice, but it does not specify which medicine to use for patients with different clinical priorities." [10] The ADA's earlier guidance, as summarised in an overview of SGLT2 inhibitor adverse events, recommended "a SGLT-2 inhibitor with demonstrated cardiovascular benefit… in patients with established atherosclerotic cardiac disease, established kidney disease, or established heart failure." [7] The recommendation has since broadened to include GLP-1 receptor agonists at the same level, reflecting the accumulating evidence for both classes.

The difference in structure is not cosmetic. KDIGO, focused on kidney outcomes, weights the renal composite evidence heavily toward SGLT2 inhibitors and encodes that weight in a first-line placement. The ADA, covering the full spectrum of glycemic management, places both classes at the same level and leaves prioritisation to clinical judgment. The *Diabetes & Metabolism Journal* review suggests how that judgment should be exercised: "SGLT2 inhibitors are preferred in people with HF or at high-risk for HF, whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists are favored for those requiring significant weight loss, ASCVD risk reduction, CKD management, or in whom mitigation of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) or steatohepatitis is a clinical priority." [14]

A risk-stratified clinical practice guideline summarised in the Nutrition Evidence Database adds another layer of nuance: the panel issued a weak recommendation against starting either SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists in adults with type 2 diabetes and three or fewer cardiovascular risk factors without established CVD or CKD, presumably reserving the drugs for those with more than three risk factors or established disease. [30] This is a departure from the traditional "add after metformin" sequence and reflects the outcome-trial framing that now governs both classes.

### The FLOW Trial and the Elevation of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

The most consequential recent update to the guideline landscape came after the FLOW trial. FLOW (Evaluate Renal Function With Semaglutide Once Weekly, 2024) was the first dedicated kidney-outcome trial of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, testing semaglutide against placebo in people with CKD and diabetes. [14] Its positive results shifted the guideline calculus. As the *Diabetes & Metabolism Journal* review states: "Based on findings from the FLOW trial, recent guidelines have elevated GLP-1 receptor agonists to the same level of recommendation as SGLT2 inhibitors for people with CKD." [14]

This elevation is significant because it closes the evidence gap that had previously separated the classes. Before FLOW, the SGLT2 inhibitor class had three dedicated kidney-outcome trials (CREDENCE, DAPA-CKD, EMPA-KIDNEY), while GLP-1 receptor agonists had only cardiovascular outcome trials with renal secondary endpoints. FLOW's success provided the primary renal evidence that allowed guidelines to place the classes on equal footing for CKD. [14] A 2026 article in *Translational Research*, cited in the review's reference list, addresses the "mechanistic complementarity of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists" in the post-FLOW era. [14]

The elevation to "the same level of recommendation" does not, however, negate the KDIGO hierarchy. The two bodies are responding to the same evidence with different framings: KDIGO's first-line placement of SGLT2 inhibitors remains in place, while the ADA's non-hierarchical recommendation now treats both classes as equally valid first-line options. [2][10] The 2026 network meta-analysis frames this as the evidence base for "a tailored, comorbidity-focused strategy for medication prescription, as advocated by recent guidelines" — the divergent efficacy profiles (SGLT2 inhibitors stronger for heart failure and renal progression; GLP-1 receptor agonists better for weight and with a lower risk of genital infections and ketoacidosis) becoming the basis for match-the-drug-to-the-patient. [4]

### The Implementation Gap: From Recommendation to Practice

The guideline recommendations, however well-founded, are not being fully implemented in routine care. The research pipeline identified under-implementation of guideline-recommended albuminuria testing and RAS inhibitor use as a persistent problem in real-world practice. [2][14] This matters because the KDIGO guidance places SGLT2 inhibitors "alongside" RAS inhibitors and multifactorial risk factor management [2] — the trial populations on which the recommendations rest largely received both. If routine care does not deliver the foundational therapies the trials assumed, the observed benefits may not translate directly.

### B14. What is the comparative risk of diabetic ketoacidosis?

Compared with GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors carry roughly a 2.4-fold higher relative risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (RR 2.36, 95% CI 1.33–4.17), and versus placebo the increase is about 2.2-fold (RR 2.24, 95% CI 1.45–3.46); GLP-1 receptor agonists show no increased risk versus placebo (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.65–1.39). The absolute risk is low: across 73,752 RCT participants there were 159 ketoacidosis events, and in CREDENCE rates were 2.2 vs 0.2 per 1,000 patient-years for canagliflozin vs placebo. Thus the comparative risk ranking is SGLT2 inhibitors > placebo ≈ GLP-1 receptor agonists.

### B17. What are the limitations of these trials (e.g., duration, patient population, direct vs indirect comparison)?

The principal limitations are the lack of direct head-to-head randomized trials, so all comparisons between SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists are indirect via placebo-controlled trials, which lowers certainty to moderate-low and precludes formal testing of inconsistency due to star-shaped networks. Patient populations differed across trials: SGLT2i trials included more patients with heart failure and worse kidney function, while GLP-1RA trials often excluded severe heart failure, challenging the transitivity assumption. Trial durations and follow-up periods varied, and some analyses excluded trials with follow-up under 1.5 years; real-world CKD populations also differ from trial participants. Heterogeneity in population characteristics, treatment duration, and follow-up, plus reliance on indirect estimates, may introduce bias and residual confounding.

- Confidence: high
- Sources: [4][10][12][14][1][6]
- Still missing: No head-to-head trials exist, so direct comparative evidence is entirely absent; individual patient data for subgroup analyses are lacking.
